Consider a commuter train travelling along its tracks, like the one shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:commuter-train}. This is a sight in every city around the world
everyday.

If the linkage between every car in the train are all connected properly and
behaving as they should, we should expect every part of the train to have the
same acceleration, velocity and displacement, i.e. the whole train moves as one
object. While this is a very simple problem to solve: the forward acceleration
of the train is caused by the static friction between the wheel connected to
the engine, when treating this problem as a single object, we will never find
out how strong the linkage need to be.

The coefficient of kinetic friction between the masses and the floor is
$\mu_k$. Here, we are interested in two unknowns:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=6pt]
\item What is the magnitude of acceleration ($a$) of the masses, assuming that
  the cable does not break?
\item What is the magnitude of the tension force ($F_\text{ten}$) in the cable?
\end{enumerate}
In order to answer these questions, we must find the forces acting on each of
the masses.
\begin{remark}
  Obviously cable cannot \emph{literally} be massless, but here, we assume
  that the mass of the cable is insignificant compared to $m_1$ and $m_2$, and
  therefore we can ignore it without making our answers inaccurate.
\end{remark}

Once the free-body diagrams are completed, we can sum the forces along the
direction of motion for each object, i.e.\ applying the second law of motion
individually on each object. In this case, the motion of the masses is entirely
horizontal, therefore the vertical component of net force for both object must
be zero:
\begin{equation*}
  \sum F_{\text{vertical},1}=0\quad\quad\sum F_{\text{vertical},2}=0
\end{equation*}
From this, we can find the magnitude of the normal forces, and the magnitude of
the kinetic friction on the two masses:
\begin{align*}
  F_{\text{norm},1}&=m_1g\quad\rightarrow\quad F_{\text{fric},1}=\mu_km_1g\\
  F_{\text{norm},2}&=m_1g\quad\rightarrow\quad F_{\text{fric},2}=\mu_km_2g
\end{align*}
(We are \emph{lucky} that the coefficient of friction is the same; in general,
we would expect a different value of $\mu_k$ for each mass.) After solving for
the vertical direction, the horizontal direction becomes:
\begin{align*}
  F_\text{app}-\underbracket[1pt]{\mu_km_1g}_{F_{\text{fric},1}}-F_\text{ten} &= m_1a\\
  F_\text{ten}-\underbracket[1pt]{\mu_km_2g}_{F_{\text{fric},2}} &= m_2a
\end{align*}
Since the cable does not break, $m_1$ and $m_2$ must have the same acceleration
$a$ towards the right, and both masses must also experience the same tension
force along the cable. Mathematically, we have two equations with two unknowns,
and this becomes a straightfoward algebra exercise. After a little bit of work
that will be left as an exercise for the reader, we find that the acceleration
of the masses is equal to
\begin{equation*}
  a=\frac F{m_1+m_2}-\mu_k g
  %\label{eq:accel}
\end{equation*}
while the tension force is equal to
\begin{equation*}
  F_\text{ten} =\frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2} F_\text{app}
\end{equation*}
If the applied force varies with time, then we expect both $a$ and $F_\text{ten}$
to also vary with time as well.

But there is also another way to view the problem as well. When we add the
equations of motion for both $m_1$ and $m_2$, we get the equation:
%Therefore we can \emph{sum} the two equations above
%(Eqs.~\ref{eq:m1}--\ref{eq:m3}) to get the expression:
\begin{equation*}
  F-\mu_kMg = Ma
\end{equation*}
where $M=m_1+m_2$ is the total mass of the system. This is just the second law
of motion applied to the whole system as a single object. Then the

  a=\frac FM-\mu g
\end{equation*}

an actual numerical value.

\subsection{Bodies Connected via Normal Forces}
Two masses ($m_1$ and $m_2$) are being pushed along a level surface by an
external applied force $\bm F_\text{app}$, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:pushed}.
\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=4/3]
    \draw[thick] (-1.5,0)--(3.5,0);
    \draw[mass] rectangle (1,1) node[midway]{$m_1$};
    \draw[mass] (1,0) rectangle (2.5,1.25) node[midway]{$m_2$};
    \draw[vector] (-1,.5)--(0,.5) node[pos=0,left]{$\bm F_\text{app}$};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Two masses pushed together by an external force}
  \label{fig:pushed}
\end{figure}
The coefficient of kinetic friction between the masses and the surface is
$\mu_k$. We want to consider the following questions:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=6pt]
\item What is the magnitude of acceleration of the masses?
\item What is the normal force that the masses exerts on each other at the
  interface?
\end{enumerate}
Like the previous problem, we start with free-body diagrams for each of the
masses, shown in Figure~\ref{fig:fbd-pushed}.

\begin{remark}
  At this time, we should point out two details about the normal forces in
  this example.
  \begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
  \item All normal forces ($\bm F_{\text{norm},1}$, $\bm F_{\text{norm},2}$ and
    $\bm F_{\text{norm},12}$) appear in the free-body diagrams as if they only act
    on the centre of mass. In fact, the normal force is spread over the entire
    contact surface.
  \item The normal force $\bm F_{\text{norm},12}$ acts at the interface between the
    two masses. It is an action-reaction pair, similar to the tension force in
    the previous example. When $m_1$ pushes against $m_2$ (action), $m_2$
    pushes back against $m_1$ with equal magnitude.
  \end{itemize}  
\end{remark}
Using the free-body digrams, we then sum forces along the direction of motion
for each mass. Again, since there is no vertical motion, the expression for
the kinetic friction forces on the masses are the same as the last example:
\begin{align*}
  F_\text{app}-F_{\text{norm},12}-\underbracket[1pt]{\mu_km_1g}_{F_{\text{fric},1}} &=m_1a\\
  F_{\text{norm},12}-\underbracket[1pt]{\mu_km_2g}_{F_{\text{fric},2}} &=m_2a
\end{align*}
Again, we recognize that both masses must have the acceleration. Adding them
together, we have
\begin{align}
  \nonumber
  F - \mu(m_1+m_2)g  &= (m_1+m_2)a\\
  F - \mu Mg  &= Ma \label{eq:system}
\end{align}
where $M=m_1+m_2$ is the total mass of the system of objects.
Eq.~\ref{eq:system} is essentially the second law of motion applied to the
entire system as if it is a single object. Solving for acceleration, we have:
\begin{equation}
  a= \frac FM- \mu g
  \label{eq:accel2}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\ref{eq:accel2} is identical to Eq.~\ref{eq:accel} from our first example.
Again, like the last problem, acceleration can be expressed as an algebraic
expression or with numerical values depending on the problem.

\textbf{Step 3--Find remaining forces:} Once acceleration is known, we can find
the remaining unknown force: the normal force $N_{12}$ between the blocks.
The magnitude of the normal force $N_{12}$ can be calculated using the
force-balance equations of either mass. Using the equation for $M_1$ and solving
for $N_{12}$:
\begin{align*}
  N_{12} &= F- m_1({\color{red}a}+\mu g)\\
  &= {\color{blue}F} - m_1\left({\color{red}\frac FM-\mu g} +\mu g\right) \\
  &= {\color{blue}M\frac FM} - m_1\left(\frac FM\right) \\
  &= \frac{M-m_1}M F\\
  N_{12}&= \frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2} F
\end{align*}
Or we can apply the same procedure to the force balance for $m_2$:
\begin{align*}
  N_{12} &= m_2 ({\color{red}a} + \mu g) \\
  &= m_2 \left({\color{red}\frac FM-\mu g} + \mu g\right) \\
  &= m_2 \left(\frac FM\right) \\
  &= \frac{m_2}{m_1+m_2} F
\end{align*}
which is the same as before.

\end{enumerate}

There are two action-reaction pairs of forces:
\begin{itemize}
\item Normal force $\bm F_{\text{norm},12}$ at the interface between $m_1$ and
  $m_2$ acts upwards on $m_1$ and downwards on $m_2$
\item Static friction\footnote{That is, if the blocks don't slide against each
other. If they do, then the friction would be kinetic friction instead}
  $\bm F_\text{fric}$,also at the interface between $m_1$ and $m_2$, acts
  forward on $m_1$, and backwards on $m_2$.
\end{itemize}

%\textbf{Step 2:}
For this problem, we first focus on the top block. We can see from the
free-body diagram that the force that pulls $m_1$ forward is the static
friction force $\bm F_\text{fric}$.
%The second law of motion applied to $m_1$ is:
Summing the forces along the direction of motion, which is entirely horizontal:
\begin{equation*}
  F_\text{fric} = m_1a
\end{equation*}
Maximum acceleration $a_\text{max}$ occurs when static friction is also at
maximum, i.e.\ $F_\text{fric}=\max F_\text{fric}=\mu_sF_{\text{norm},12}=\mu_s m_1g$:
\begin{align*}
  \mu_s m_1g &= m_1a_\text{max}\\
  a_\text{max} &=\mu_s g
\end{align*}
We see that the maximum acceleration is, in fact, independent of mass.

%\textbf{Step 3:}
Once the $a_\text{max}$ is known, we can use it to calculate the maximum applied
force. We can do this by substituting the expression for acceleration into the
force balance for $m_2$:
\begin{align*}
  F_\text{net}=F_\text{max}-\max F_\text{fric} &= m_2a_\text{max}\\
  F_\text{max} &= m_2a_\text{max}+\max F_\text{fric}\\
  &= m_2(\mu_sg)+\mu_s m_1g\\
  F_\text{max} &=\mu_s(m_1+m_2)g
\end{align*}
Or treat the whole system as a single object with total mass of $m_1+m_2$:
\begin{equation*}
  F_\text{max} = (m_1+m_2)a_\text{max}= \mu_s(m_1+m_2)g
\end{equation*}
Of course both ways gives you the same answer.
