example, uniform circular motion.}.

\section{Horizontal Spring-Mass System}

In an ideal horizontal-spring mass system, We consider a mass that rests on a
horizontal surface . The mass is connected horizontally to an ideal spring that
obeys Hooke's law perfectly, and is free to move horizontally without friction,
drag, or other damping forces. Three forces act on the mass:
gravitational force $\bm F_g=m\bm g$, normal force $\bm F_n$ at the interface
between the mass and the surface, and spring force $\bm F_s$ from the
stretched/compressed spring, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:horizontal-spring-mass1}

Since there is no vertical motion, $\sum\bm F_\text{vertical}=0$, $\bm F_g$ and
$\bm F_n$ cancel out. Net force is due only to spring force $\bm F_s=-k\bm x$
along the horizontal direction. This is true regardless of whether the spring
is in compression or in extension. (It should be obvious that the spring in
Figure~\ref{fig:horizontal-spring-mass1} is in extension.) The spring force
$\bm F_s$ is called a \emph{restoring force} because is always points towards
the equilibrium position.

Applying second law of motion ($\bm F_\text{net}=m\bm a$) along the horizontal
direction, we can equate the spring force to the mass's horizontal acceleration
($a=\diff[2] xt$) by a \emph{second-order ordinary differential equation with
constant coefficients}:
\begin{equation}
  -kx =m\diff[2]xt
  %\underbrace{-kx}_{=F_\text{net}=F_s} =m\diff[2]xt
  \label{eq:ode0}
\end{equation}
In many math textbooks, Eq.~\ref{eq:ode1} would be written in standard form:
\begin{important-equation}
  \diff[2]xt + \frac kmx=0
  \label{eq:ode1}
\end{important-equation}

Eq.~\ref{eq:ode1} is a standard problem in calculus. If you are unfamiliar with
such an equation in your calculus courses, we are solving for the position as a
function of time, i.e.\ $x(t)$. When we compare the left- and right-hand side
of Eq.~\ref{eq:ode0}, we conclude that $x(t)$ must be a function with a second
time derivative $\diff[2]xt$ that \emph{looks like} $x(t)$, but with a negative
sign, and some parameters that relate to the spring constant $k$ and mass $m$.
Only two functions can satisfy this requirement: the sinusoidal functions
$\sin(t)$ and $\cos(t)$. This means that the motion along the horizontal
direction \emph{must} be periodic.

We start with the general form of the solution:
\begin{important-equation}
  \quad x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\theta_0)
  \label{eq:gen-form}
\end{important-equation}
where $A$ is the amplitude of the oscillation, $\omega$ is the angular
frequency of the motion, called the \textbf{natural frequency}, and $\theta_0$
is a phase constant based on initial condition. For the remainder of the
analysis, we will primarily use the cosine function for simplicity. Motion in
the form of Eq.~\ref{eq:gen-form} is called the \textbf{simple harmonic motion}
(\textbf{SHM}), and such a system is called a
\textbf{simple harmonic oscillator}.

Taking the time derivative of Eq.~\ref{eq:gen-form} give us the velocity $v(t)$
of the mass:
\begin{important-equation}
  v(t)=\diff xt=-A\omega\sin(\omega t+\theta_0)
\end{important-equation}
And the second derivative of $x(t)$ gives us acceleration $a(t)$:
\begin{important-equation}
  a(t)=\diff[2] xt=-A\omega^2\cos(\omega t+\theta_0) %=-\omega^2x
  \label{eq:shm-acceleration}
\end{important-equation}
When we compare Eq.~\ref{eq:shm-acceleration} with Eq.~\ref{eq:gen-form}, we
can quickly note that acceleration is related to position $x(t)$ by a constant
$-\omega^2$:
\begin{equation}
  a(t)=-\omega^2x(t)
  %\label{eq:shm-acceleration}
\end{equation}
Substituting expressions of $x(t)$ and $a(t)$ back into Eq.~\ref{eq:ode1}, we
find that the differential equation is satisfied if the natural frequency $\omega$ is related to
the spring constant $k$ and mass $m$ by:
\begin{important-equation}
  \omega=\sqrt{\frac km}
\end{important-equation}

\begin{remark}
  In calculus classes, you should definitely be taught that the solution to
  \emph{any} ordinary differential equation is the linear combination of
  \emph{all} possible solutions. Since both sine and cosine functions are
  possible solutions to Eq.~\ref{eq:ode1}, the actual solution $x(t)$ must be
  in then form:
  \begin{equation*}
    x(t)=c_1\cos(\omega t)+c_2\sin(\omega t)
  \end{equation*}
  where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are coefficients based on initial position and velocity
  $x(0)$ and $v(0)$. We can use a trigonometric identity to show that
  Eq.~\ref{eq:gen-form} is identical to the form of solution above:
  \begin{align*}
    x(t)&=A\cos(\omega t+\theta_0)\\
    &=A\left[\cos(\omega t)\cos(\theta_0)+\sin(\omega t)\sin(\theta_0)\right]\\
    &=\underbracket[1pt]{A\cos(\theta_0)}_{c_1}\cos(\omega t)
    +\underbracket[1pt]{A\sin(\theta_0)}_{c_2}\sin(\omega t)
  \end{align*}
  From an even broader discussions on mathematics, which is not part of the
  physics discussion here---so we will be very brief here---all possible
  solutions to the ordinary differential equation are, in fact, functions that
  are \emph{orthogonal} to each other. Two functions $f(x)$ and $g(x)$ are
  orthogonal when
  \begin{equation*}
    \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)g(x)\dl x=0 
  \end{equation*}
  Much like the basis vectors defining a vector space (for example $\iii$,
  $\jjj$ and $\kkk$ directions representing the $x$, $y$ and $z$ axes) are
  orthogonal, and any vector can be represented as a linear combination of the
  basis vectors, the orthogonal functions are treated as basis vectors and the
  solution is a vector in this \emph{functional} space.
\end{remark}

\textbf{But when should we use cosine, and when should be use sine?} Both
functions can be solutions to Eq.~\ref{eq:ode1}, so choosing the ``best''
function is down to the initial condition (i.e.\ when motion begins at $t=0$).
In general, we prefer a function such that we don't need a phase constant, i.e.\
$\theta_0=0$. If oscillation begins at amplitude $x=A$, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:start-from-A}, the cosine function is preferred. If the spring
is initially compressed to $x=-A$, we can use the negative cosine function
instead.

Conversely, if the mass is given a ``tap'' in the ($+$) direction at $t=0$ to
give it an initial velocity of $v=v_\text{max}$, then the sine function is
preferred, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:start-from-0}. In this case, the
oscillator has an initial position of $x(0)=0$. For sine function, we would use
the following functions of time for the mass's position, velocity and
acceleration:
\begin{align*}
  x(t)&=A\sin(\omega t)\\
  v(t)&=A\omega\cos(\omega t)\\
  a(t)&=-A\omega^2\sin(\omega t)
\end{align*}
Mathematically, the two functions only differ by a phase constant of
$\dfrac{\pi}2$. In practice, however, we must remember that it is entirely
possible that a simple harmonic oscillator can have initial conditions where
$x(0)\neq 0$ and $v(0)\neq 0$.

Since the motion of a simple harmonic oscillator is strictly periodic, the
frequency ($f$)---the number of oscillations per second, measured in
\emph{hertz} (\si\hertz)---of its motion is related to the angular frequency
$\omega$ by:
\begin{important-equation}
  f=\frac\omega{2\pi}=\frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac km}
\end{important-equation}
%We should note that
Depending on context or application, the term ``natural frequency'' may refer
to either $\omega$ or $f$. Usually it is easy to know from the context of the
book or journal, or even verbal discussion.

The period ($T$, measured in seconds) is the reciprocal of the frequency, given
by:
\begin{important-equation}
  T=\frac1f=2\pi\sqrt{\frac mk}
\end{important-equation}
It should be clear that angular frequency ($\omega$), frequency ($f$), and
period ($T$) are all independent of amplitude $A$.

\begin{remark}
  \textbf{We keep referring to the natural frequency of the simple harmonic
    motion as its ``angular frequency''. Is $\omega$ the same angular velocity
    term from circular motion?}

  Yes, it most certainly is! Consider an object
  moving in uniform circular motion about the origin of the $xy$ plane with
  angular velocity $\omega$. Here, the angular position of the object would be
  described by
  \begin{equation*}
    \theta(t)=\omega t+\theta_0
  \end{equation*}
  where $\theta_0$ is the initial angular position of the object. The $x$ and
  $y$ coordinates of this motion are, respectively,
  \begin{align*}
    x(t) &=r\cos(\theta)=r\cos(\omega t+\theta_0)\\
    y(t) &=r\sin(\theta)=r\sin(\omega t+\theta_0)
  \end{align*}
  Both of $x(t)$ and $y(t)$ are in the same form as Eq.~\ref{eq:ode1}. In
  essence, a simple harmonic motion is the \emph{projection} of a uniform
  circular motion onto an axis. The ``radius'' of that circular motion is the
  amplitude $A$ of the simple harmonic motion.

  \end{center}   
\end{remark}


\begin{remark}
  \textbf{Are sine and cosine functions our only options?} Another function
  that we can try as a solution to Eq.~\ref{eq:ode1} is the exponential
  function $x=e^t$, where its derivatives are related to the function itself.
  Again, we start with a general form and take the first two time derivatives:
  \begin{equation*}
    x(t)=Ae^{\omega t}\quad\longrightarrow\quad
    \diff xt=A\omega e^{\omega t}\quad\longrightarrow\quad
    \diff[2] xt=A\omega^2 e^{\omega t}=\omega^2x
  \end{equation*}
  It is clear that the problem with using this function is that second
  derivative is missing the negative sign. However, if the exponential function
  is \emph{complex}, i.e.:
  \begin{equation*}
    x(t)=Ae^{i\omega t}
  \end{equation*}
  Then the second derivative \emph{does} in fact have the negative sign:
  \begin{equation*}
    \diff xt=iA\omega e^{i\omega t}\quad\longrightarrow\quad
    \diff[2] xt=i^2A\omega^2 e^{i\omega t}=-\omega^2 e^{i\omega t}=-\omega^2x
  \end{equation*}
  This should not come as a surprise, since the complex exponential function
  and the sinusoidal functions are related:
  \begin{equation*}
    Ae^{i\omega t}=A\left[\cos(\omega t)+i\sin(\omega t)\right]
  \end{equation*}
  A further exploration of the mathematics is presented in
  Appendix~\ref{appendix:harmonic-motion}.
\end{remark}




\subsection{Energies in the Oscillator}

As the mass oscillates, the kinetic energy of the mass is
\begin{equation}
  K=\frac12mv^2=\frac12m\left[-A\omega\sin(\omega t)\right]^2
\end{equation}
Multiplying out the terms and substituting for $\omega^2=\frac km$, we have:
\begin{important-equation}
  K=\frac12kA^2\sin^2(\omega t)
\end{important-equation}
Similarly, we can calculate the elastic potential energy stored in the spring
during the oscillation:
\begin{equation}
  U_e=\frac12kx^2=\frac12k\left[A\cos(\omega t)\right]^2
\end{equation}
which gives us:
\begin{important-equation}
  U_e=\frac12kA^2\cos^2(\omega t)
\end{important-equation}
The total energy of the system, which is just the sum of the kinetic and
elastic potential energies, is not surprisingly:
\begin{important-equation}
  E_\text{total}=K+U_e
  =\frac12kA^2\left[\sin^2(\omega t)+\cos^2(\omega t)\right]=\frac12kA^2
\end{important-equation}
We can plot the functions over time, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:total-energy}.

\section{Vertical Spring-Mass System}

In a vertical spring-mass systems, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:vertical-spring-mass}, a mass is suspended vertically from a
fixed point by an ideal spring. Again, we assume that there is no friction, no
drag or other damping forces in the system. The difference between a horizontal
and vertical system is that here, we must also consider the gravitational force
$\bm F_g=m\bm g$ which acts along the direction of motion. The presence of
gravity also shifts the equilibrium position downwards by a distance $B$ from
the unstretched length of the spring.

    \draw[vector,red] (1,2)--(1,2.8) node[right]{$\bm F_s$};
    \fill[red] (1,2) circle (.05);
    \draw[axes] (1,1)--(1,.5) node[below]{$+$};
    \draw[vector] (.3,4)--(.3,2.3) node[midway,left]{$x$};
    \draw[vector] (1.7,4)--(1.7,3.5) node[midway,right]{$B$};
    \begin{scope}[thick,dashed]
      \draw (0,4)--(2,4) node[right]{\scriptsize unstretched};
      \draw (0,3.5)--(2,3.5) node[right]{\scriptsize equilibrium};

  \caption{A vertical spring-mass system with no friction and damping force}
  \label{fig:vertical-spring-mass}

Using the downward direction as the positive direction of motion, the second
law of motion becomes:
\begin{equation}
  mg-kx=m\diff[2]xt
  %\label{eq:ode2}
\end{equation}
Or, written standard form:
\begin{important-equation}
  \diff[2]xt+\frac kmx-g=0
  \label{eq:ode-vertical}
\end{important-equation}
The only notable difference between \ref{eq:ode-vertical} and \ref{eq:ode1} is
the presence of the gravitational force $\bm F_g=m\bm g$. But $\bm F_g$ is a
constant force, the only change in the position function $x(t)$ compared to the
horizontal system is the addition of a constant $B$, is the downwards shift of

In applying the law of conservation of energy in Chapter~\ref{chapter:energy},
we have shown that, in the absence of any friction, drag, or damping forces,
the total mechanical energy of the simple pendulum system is constant:
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta U_g + \Delta K = 0
\end{equation*}
In studying the vertical circular motion in Chapter~\ref{chapter:circ-motion},
we have also shown that when the mass is deflected by an angle $\theta$, there
is a tangential force
\begin{equation*}
  F_t=-mg\sin\theta
\end{equation*}
We have added a negative sign to account for the sign convention for circular
motion; if $\theta$ is counter clockwise, in the positive direction, then $F_t$
is clockwise, in the negative direction.  As we are interested in the
pendulum's motion in the angular direction, there is no need to worry about the
radial direction; it does not have to do with the restoring force.

Substitute $F_t$ into the second law of motion:
\begin{equation}
  F_t=ma_t\quad\rightarrow\quad
  -mg\sin\theta=m\ell\diff[2]{\theta}t
\end{equation}
After cancelling the mass term $m$, we are left with a fairly difficult
differential equation:
\begin{equation}
  -g\sin\theta=\ell\diff[2]{\theta}t
\end{equation}
Solving this differential equation is very difficult because of the
$\sin\theta$ term. However, we can simplify the problem by using the Taylor
series expansion of the sine function:
\begin{equation*}
  \sin\theta
  =\theta-\frac{\theta^3}{3!}+\frac{\theta^5}{5!}-\frac{\theta^7}{7!}+\cdots
\end{equation*}
For small angles of $\theta$, we can use the \emph{small-angle approximation},
where
\begin{equation*}
  \sin\theta\approx\theta
\end{equation*}
and the differential equation reduces to the same form as the horizontal
spring-mass system that we have studied in Eq.~\ref{eq:ode1}:
\begin{equation}
  -\frac g\ell\theta = \diff[2]{\theta}t
\end{equation}
For small angles of deflection, the restoring force is approximately
proportional to the angular displacement about the pivot, measured from the
bottom of the swing, just like Hooke's law. Therefore the solution for
$\theta(t)$ is also a sinusoidal function, like the horizontal spring-mass
system:
\begin{important-equation}
  \theta(t)=\theta_\text{max}\cos(\omega t+\varphi)
  \quad\quad\text{simple pendulum, small angles}
\end{important-equation}
where $\theta_\text{max}$ is the maximum deflection of the pendulum (i.e.\ the
amplitude)---which should be kept to a small angle for the solution to be
valid---and $\varphi$ is a phase shift based on the initial condition of the
pendulum. Again, whether the solution for $\theta(t)$ should be a cosine or a
sine function depends on the initial condition. We want to express the motion
without worrying about the phase constant, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:pendulum-initial}.

The natural frequency of the oscillation $\omega$ is given by:
\begin{important-equation}   
  \omega=\sqrt{\frac g\ell}\quad\text{(simple pendulum, small angle)}
\end{important-equation}
Consequently, the frequency of the oscillation, measured in \emph{hertz}, is
given by:
\begin{important-equation}   
  f=\frac\omega{2\pi}=
  \frac1{2\pi}\sqrt{\frac g\ell}
  \quad\text{(simple pendulum, small angle)}
\end{important-equation}
while the period of the oscillation, measured in \emph{seconds}, is the inverse
of the frequency:
\begin{important-equation}   
  T=\frac1f=
  2\pi\sqrt{\frac\ell{g}}
  \quad\text{(simple pendulum, small angle)}
\end{important-equation}

\subsection{Velocity and Acceleration}

We can take the time derivative of $\theta(t)$ to find the angular velocity and
velocity about the pivot of the pendulum:
\begin{important-equation}
  \diff{\theta}t=-\theta_\text{max}\omega\sin(\omega t)
  \quad\to\quad
  v(t)=\ell\diff{\theta}t=-\ell\theta_\text{max}\omega\sin(\omega t)
\end{important-equation}
And the second time derivative gives us the angular acceleration and and
tangential acceleration about the pivot:
\begin{important-equation}
  \diff[2]{\theta}t=-\theta_\text{max}\omega^2\cos(\omega t)
  \quad\to\quad
  a_t(t)=\ell\diff[2]{\theta}t=-\ell\theta_\text{max}\omega^2\cos(\omega t)
\end{important-equation}

\section{Damped Oscillator}

In reality, there are friction, drag, or other damping forces present in the
spring-mass system, taking energy away from the system. We can represent the
energy dissipation in a horizontal spring-mass system schematically by the shock
absorber, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:damping1}. The shock absorber exerts a
``damping force'' $\bm F_D$ on the mass.

This damping force is typically related to velocity, but acting the opposite
direction the motion of the mass, and we can write it in general form as:
\begin{equation}
  F_D=-bv^n
\end{equation}
where $b$ is a positive constant called the \textbf{damping factor}. For
kinetic friction, which does not depend on velocity, we can use $n=0$; for
viscous damping in a practical shock absorber, $n\approx 1$, while for
aerodynamic drag, $n=2$, as seen in Chapter~\ref{chapter:dynamics}. For the
purpose of our analysis, we will use $n=1$ to \emph{approximate} the
combined effects of these three forces. Of course, whether this is actually
accurate will depends on specific problems.
%Of course our choice of using $n=1$ will not be entirely correct
%for every problem, but it is \emph{sufficiently} accurate for a wide range of
%situations.}

With the addition of the damping force, the differential equation is obtained
again by applying second law of motion, but this time with the additional term
from the damping force, highlighted in red:
\begin{equation*}
  \sum F = {\color{blue}F_s} + {\color{red}F_D} = m{\color{orange}a}
  \quad\longrightarrow\quad
  -{\color{blue}kx}-{\color{red}b\diff xt} = m{\color{orange}\diff[2]xt}
\end{equation*}
The equation can be arranged into standard form:
\begin{important-equation}  
  \diff[2]xt+\frac bm\diff xt+\frac kmx=0
  \label{eq:ode2}
\end{important-equation}
The solution to Eq.~\ref{eq:ode2} is still relatively straightforward, although
not as simple as the simple harmonic oscillator.



\subsection{Under-damped Oscillator}
For small values of $b$ (we will find out that this means that $b<\sqrt{4mk}$),
the solution to Eq.~\ref{eq:ode2} has both an exponential decay term and a
sinusoidal (oscillatory) term:
\begin{important-equation}
  x(t)=
  \underbracket[1pt]{Ae^{-\frac{bt}{2m}}}_\text{amplitude}
  \cos(\omega't+\theta_0)
  \quad\text{(damped oscillator)}
  \label{eq:damped-solution}
\end{important-equation}
%where
%\begin{displaymath}
%  \lambda=\frac b{2m}
%\end{displaymath}
We can see in Appendix~\ref{appendix:harmonic-motion} how the coefficient $A$
is related to the  initial amplitude. Like the simple
harmonic oscillator, $A$, $\theta_0$, and whether to use a sine or cosine
function, are based on initial position $x(0)$ and initial velocity $v(0)$. The
motion of the mass is called a \textbf{damped harmonic motion}, and such a
system is called a \textbf{damped harmonic oscillator}.

Unlike the simple harmonic oscillator, the motion of the damped oscillator is
\emph{quasi}-periodic because the oscillation pattern is not perfectly
repeated. The natural frequency\footnote{Because the motion is quasi-periodic,
this frequency should be properly called a ``quasi-frequency''.} $\omega'$ for
the damped oscillator is shifted from the undamped case $\omega$ based on the
damping factor $b$:
\begin{important-equation}
  \omega'=\sqrt{\omega^2-\left(\frac b{2m}\right)^2}
  \quad\text{where}\quad
  \omega=\sqrt{\frac km}
  %\quad\text{and}\quad
  %\lambda=\frac b{2m}
  \label{damping1}
\end{important-equation}
Note that $\omega'<\omega$ (natural frequency decreases) because of the
damping factor $b$.



\subsection{Critical Damping}

As the damping factor $b$ increases, the damped natural frequency $\omega'$
continues to decrease until it reaches \textbf{critical damping}, when
$\omega'=0$
%zero, which we can calculate by setting $\omega'=0$ in Eq.~\ref{damping1}, and
%solving for the resulting damping constant, which is called the 
\begin{equation*}
  \omega'=\sqrt{\omega^2-\left(\frac{b_c}{2m}\right)^2}=0
  %\quad\text{where}\quad
  %\lambda_c=\frac{b_c}{2m}
\end{equation*}
We can solve for the \textbf{critical damping factor} $b_c$ when critical
damping occurs:
\begin{important-equation}
  b_c=2m\omega=\sqrt{4mk}
\end{important-equation}
At critical damping, the solution to Eq.~\ref{eq:ode2} for the position of the
mass $x(t)$ no longer has any sinusoidal terms, but is instead a linear
combination of two exponential decay functions related to $\lambda_c$:
\begin{important-equation}
  x(t)=(c_1+c_2t)e^{\lambda_ct}
  \quad\text{(critically damped motion)}
  \label{eq:critically-damped-solution}
\end{important-equation}
where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are determined by initial position and velocity. If a
mass is released at amplitude $A_0$ from rest (i.e.\ $x(0)=A_0$,
$\dot x(0)=0$), then Eq.~\ref{eq:critically-damped-solution} becomes:
\begin{equation*}
  x(t)=A_0\left[1+\lambda_ct\right]e^{-\lambda_ct}
  \label{eq:critically-damped-solution-2}
\end{equation*}
The derivation of the solution and the corresponding coefficients are shown in
details in Appendix~\ref{appendix:harmonic-motion}.
\begin{remark}
  The solution to the critically-damped motion in
  Eq.~\ref{eq:critically-damped-solution} are really two linearly independent
  solutions:
  \begin{align*}
    x_1(t) &= c_1e^{\lambda_ct} \\
    x_2(t) &= c_2te^{\lambda_ct}
  \end{align*}
  We should not be surprised that both solutions are also orthogonal functions.
\end{remark}
A critically damped system returns to its equilibrium position in the shortest
time with \emph{no} oscillation. Critical or near-critical damping is desired
in many engineering designs (e.g.\ shock absorbers on car suspensions).



\subsection{Over-damped System}

When $b>b_c$, the motion of the mass is primarily driven by the damping force
instead of the spring force. The system now becomes \textbf{over-damped}. In
such a case, the solution Eq.~\ref{eq:ode2} is the linear combination of two
very slow exponential decay functions without oscillation:
\begin{important-equation}
  x(t)=c_1e^{r_1t}+c_2e^{r_2t}
  \quad\text{(Over-damped motion)}
  \label{eq:over-damped-solution}
\end{important-equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
  r_1=\frac{-b+\sqrt{b^2-4mk}}{2m}\quad
  r_2=\frac{-b-\sqrt{b^2-4mk}}{2m}\quad
\end{equation*}
We can easily verify that both solutions are real and negative. Once again, the
constants $c_1$ and $c_2$ are based on initial position and velocity of the
mass.

\subsection{Energy in a Damped System}
The non-conservative damping force dissipates energy from the oscillator at
a rate of:
\begin{equation}
  P=\diff Et=\bm F_D\cdot\bm v=-bv^2
\end{equation}
As velocity relate to energy by: $(v_{av})^2=E/m$, power dissipation is a
first-order linear differential equation:
\begin{equation}
  \diff Et=-\frac bmE
\end{equation}
The solution to the differential equation shows the total amount of energy decreases
exponentially with time:
\begin{equation}
  E(t)=E_0e^{-\frac bmt} %=E_0e^{-\frac{t}{\tau}}
\end{equation}




\section{Driven Oscillators}

To keep a damped system going, energy must be added into the system. That means
that positive external work must be done to the system. To ensure that this is
the case, we subject the system to an external forcing function
$\bm F_\text{ext}(t)$ (the ``external applied force'' shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:driven1}) along the direction of motion that is harmonic with
time, with a driving frequency of $\omega_\text{ext}$:
\begin{equation}
  F_\text{ext}=F\cos(\omega_\text{ext}t)
\end{equation}
In general, the driving frequency $\omega_\text{ext}$ is unrelated to the
undamped natural frequency $\omega$ or the damped quasi-frequency $\omega'$,
i.e.\ we assume that $\omega_\text{ext}\neq\omega\neq\omega'$.

Again, the second-order ordinary differential equation is obtained by
applying the second law of motion:
\begin{equation}
  \sum F=
  \underbracket[1pt]{-kx}_{F_s}\underbracket[1pt]{-bv}_{F_D}+
  \underbracket[1pt]{F\cos(\omega_\text{ext}t)}_{F_\text{ext}}=ma
\end{equation}
In standard form, the differential equation is written as:
\begin{important-equation}
  m\diff[2]xt + b\diff xt+kx=F\cos(\omega_\text{ext}t)
  \label{eq:ode3}
\end{important-equation}
The differential equation is similarly to the damped harmonic oscillator: the only difference
between Eq.~\ref{eq:ode2} and Eq.~\ref{eq:ode3} is the additional forcing
function term on the right-hand side. This is a \emph{much} more difficult
problem in calculus, but nevertheless still a standard problem that is usually
taught in a dedicated differential equation course in university.
The solution to Eq.~\ref{eq:ode3} contains two parts:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=8pt]
\item A \textbf{transient solution} that is obtained by setting
  $F_\text{ext}=0$. Essentially this is the solution for the damped harmonic
  oscillator
  case, and the solution is either an under-damped, critically-damped, or
  over-damped motion. The transient solution depends on the initial position
  and velocity, but regardless of the damping factor $b$, the solution will
  become negligible over time.
  
\item A \textbf{steady-state solution} that does not depend on the initial
  condition, \emph{only} on the forcing function $\bm F_\text{ext}$. Solving for
  the steady-state solution will be left as a difficult calculus exercise.
  We will instead focus on the solution.
\end{itemize}
The steady-state solution to the driven oscillator is a harmonic motion at the
driving frequency $\omega_\text{ext}$ of the external force:
\begin{important-equation}
  x(t)=A\cos(\omega_\text{ext}t+\theta)
  \quad\text{(driven oscillator)}
  \label{eq:gen-solution3}
\end{important-equation}
%(For those who are interested, Appendix X shows how the steady-state solution
%is derived.)
The amplitude of the driven oscillation $A$ depends on both the natural
frequency of the simple harmonic oscillator $\omega$ (which is a constant for
any specific system), and the driving frequency $\omega_\text{ext}$ of the
forcing function:
\begin{important-equation}
  A=\frac F{\sqrt{m^2(\omega^2-\omega_\text{ext}^2)^2+b^2\omega_\text{ext}^2}}
  \label{eq:amplitude1}
\end{important-equation}
In other words, the amplitude of the driven oscillator is a function of the
driving frequency, i.e.\ $A=A(\omega_\text{ext})$.
%And the phase contant is given by:
%\begin{equation}
%  \tan\theta_0=\frac{b\omega_\ext{ext}}{m(\omega^2-\omega_a^2)}
%\end{equation}
Maximum amplitude $A=A_\text{max}$ occurs when $\diff A{\omega_\text{ext}}=0$,
which is a bit difficult and messy to solve. Instead, we can accomplish the
same goal by minimizing the  denominator in Eq.~\ref{eq:amplitude1}, i.e.\
taking the derivative of the denominator only with respect to external
frequency $\omega_\text{ext}$, and setting the derivative to 0:
\begin{equation*}
  \diff{}{\omega_\text{ext}}
  \left[m^2(\omega^2-\omega_\text{ext}\text{ext}^2)^2+b^2\omega_\text{ext}^2\right]
  =0
\end{equation*}
Taking the derivative and solving for $\omega_\text{ext}$, we find that maximum
amplitude $A_\text{max}$ occurs when the driving frequency is
\emph{approximately} equal to the damped natural frequency:
%i.e.\ $\omega_a\approx\omega'$.
\begin{important-equation}
  \omega_\text{ext}=\sqrt{\omega^2-\frac{b^2}{2m^2}}\approx\omega'
\end{important-equation}
Note that the terms inside the square root is \emph{not} the same as in
Eq.~\ref{damping1}!

%maximum amplitude occurs at a frequency that is very close to (albeit slightly
%different from)

It shows that, for a lightly-damped system (i.e.\ damping factor $b$ much
smaller than critical), amplitude response is greatest when
$\omega_\text{ext}\approx\omega'\approx\omega$, and that lower the damping
factor $b$, the higher and narrower the peak is. But as the damping factor
increases, and the damping force begin to dominate, the maximum amplitude will
occur at lower and lower frequencies. Once the driven oscillator has reached
its maximum amplitude for that driving frequency, the power delivered to the
system by the forcing function will be equal to the power dissipated by the
damping force(s).

The phase constant $\theta$ in Eq.\ref{eq:gen-solution3} also depends on the
driving frequency $\omega_\text{ext}$.
\begin{important-equation}
  \tan\theta=\frac{b\omega_\text{ext}}{m(\omega^2-\omega_\text{ext}^2)}
  \label{eq:forced-oscillator-phase-shift}
\end{important-equation}
When $\omega_\text{ext}=\omega$ is substituted into the phase shift expression,
the right-hand side becomes undefined. From this, we obtain a phase shift of
$\theta=\pi/2$. Taking derivative of $x(t)$ for velocity $v(t)$, and
substituting $\theta=\pi/2$:
\begin{equation}
  v(t)=\dot x
  =-A\omega_\text{ext}\sin(\omega_\text{ext}t-\frac{\pi}2)
  =A\omega_\text{ext}\cos(\omega_\text{ext}t)
\end{equation}
At resonance, the object is always moving in the same direction as the
driving force:
\begin{align*}
  v(t)&=A\omega_\text{ext}\cos(\omega_\text{ext}t)\\
  F_\text{ext}(t)&=F\cos(\omega_\text{ext}t)
\end{align*}
This also makes sense from a work-energy perspective, because now the external
force is doing positive work to the system.
