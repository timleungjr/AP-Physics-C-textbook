\chapter{Mathematical Considerations for Harmonic Motion Equation}
\label{appendix:harmonic-motion}

In this appendix, we will do deep dive into the vigorious mathematics of how
the equations for the undamped simple harmonic oscillator, the under-damped
oscillator, the critically damped and overdamped motions in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:harmonic-motion} are derived. For \emph{some}
of you, this analysis may be familiar, but this is generally not a topic of
discussion for most physics or mathematics courses at this level, so most
students may find this slightly difficult. That said, as long as you have a
decent background in calculus, this should be very much understandable.

The second-order ordinary differential equation that we are solving is based on
the second law of motion, and the damping and spring forces acting on a
spring-mass system:
\begin{equation}
  m\ddot x+b\dot x+kx=0
  \label{eq:general-ode}
\end{equation}
%If the problem is a simple harmonic oscillator, we would expect $b=0$.
The kinematics and dynamics of this problem has already been discussed quite
extensively in Chapter~\ref{chapter:harmonic-motion}. Here, we are interested in
the mathematics of how we arrived at all the solutions discussed in that
chapter.

To begin, we note that Eq.~\ref{eq:general-ode} can only be satisfied if the
function $x(t)$, its first derivative $\dot x(t)$, and second derivative
$\ddot x(t)$ all have the same form. The \emph{only} function that can satisfy
this is the exponential function. This means that our solution must be a
variation of the form:
\begin{equation}
  x(t)=e^{rt}
\end{equation}
where $r$ is a parameter based on mass $m$, spring constant $k$ and damping
factor $b$. When we take the first and second derivatives of $x(t)$ with
respect to time, we have
\begin{equation*}
  \dot x=re^{rt}\quad\text{and}\quad\ddot x=r^2e^{rt}
\end{equation*}
Substituting $\dot x$ and $\ddot x$ back into Eq.~\ref{eq:general-ode}, we have
the equation:
\begin{equation}
  (mr^2+br+k)e^{rt}=0
\end{equation}
Since $e^{rt}$ must always be nonzero, we are actually solving for the roots of
the \textbf{characteristic equation}:
\begin{equation}
  mr^2+br+k=0
\end{equation}
which is just quadratic equation in $r$; the roots can be found using the
quadratic formula:
\begin{equation}
  r=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4mk}}{2m}
\end{equation}
We can see from the square root term in the above equation that:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item When the system has no damping ($b=0$), the roots are entirely imaginary.
  This corresponds to the simple harmonic oscillator.
\item When the system is lightly damped ($b<\sqrt{4mk}$), the
  roots are complex. This corresponds to the damped harmonic oscillator.
\item When the system is critically damped, ($b=\sqrt{4mk}$), there is only one
  repeating root. There is no longer a complex part, and the motion is an
  exponential decay.
\item When the system is over-damped ($b>\sqrt{4mk}$), both roots are real and
  negative. This corresponds to a slow exponential decay.
\end{itemize}



\section{Simple Harmonic Oscillator}
For the simple harmonic oscillator with no damping ($b=0$), the two roots of the
characteristic equation are related to the angular frequency of the oscillation:
\begin{equation*}
  r=\pm\frac{\sqrt{-4mk}}{2m}=\pm i\frac{\sqrt{4mk}}{\sqrt{4m^2}}
  =\pm i\underbracket[1pt]{\sqrt{\frac km}}_\omega
\end{equation*}
We have two distinct solutions based on the two imaginary roots to the
characteristic equation:
\begin{align*}
  x_1(t) &= e^{i\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)+i\sin(\omega t)\\
  x_2(t) &= e^{-i\omega t}=\cos(\omega t)-i\sin(\omega t)
\end{align*}
As is the case for any differential equations, the actual physical motion for
$x(t)$ is the \emph{linear combination} all the possibe solutions to the ODE,
i.e.:
\begin{equation*}
  x(t)=ax_1(t)+bx_2(t)
\end{equation*}
But the two solutions in their current form are awkward to work with, so
instead, we define two intermediate functions $u(t)$ and $v(t)$ as the sum and
difference of the $x_1$ and $x_2$ so that we can separate sine and cosine terms:
\begin{align*}
  u(t)=x_1(t)+x_2(t) &=2\cos(\omega t)\\
  v(t)=x_1(t)-x_2(t) &=2i\sin(\omega t)
\end{align*}
We can ignore the constant $2$ and $2i$ because they are just that: constants.
Our final solution is then a linear combination of these two intermediate
functions $u$ and $v$, i.e.:
\begin{equation}
  x(t)=c_1\cos(\omega t)+c_2\sin(\omega t)
\end{equation}
where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are constants subject to initial position $x(0)$ and
velocity $\dot x(0)$. As we have already seen in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:harmonic-motion}, this solution can also be written in
the more practical form:
\begin{important-equation}
  x(t)=A\cos(\omega t+\theta_0)\quad\text{where}\quad \omega=\frac km
\end{important-equation}
In this form, it is amplitude $A$ and phase constants $\theta_0$ that are
subject to initial position and velocity. The physics and some of the
mathematics have already been discussed in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:harmonic-motion}.




\section{Under-Damped Oscillator}

For the under-damped oscillator with damping constant $b<\sqrt{4mk}$, there
are two distinct \emph{complex} roots to the characteristic equation:
\begin{equation*}
  r=\frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4mk}}{2m} =
  -\frac b{2m} \pm i\frac{\sqrt{4mk-b^2}}{\sqrt{4m^2}} =
  -\underbracket[1pt]{\frac b{2m}}_\lambda
  \pm i\sqrt{
    \underbracket[1pt]{\frac km}_{\omega^2} -
    \underbracket[1pt]{\frac{b^2}{4m^2}}_{\lambda^2}
  }
  = \lambda\pm i\underbracket[1pt]{\sqrt{\omega^2-\lambda^2}}_{\omega'}
\end{equation*}
Again, this leads to two distinct solutions:
\begin{align*}
  x_1(t) &= e^{(-\lambda+i\omega')t}=e^{-\lambda t}[\cos(\omega't)+i\sin(\omega't)]\\
  x_2(t) &= e^{(-\lambda-i\omega')t}=e^{-\lambda t}[\cos(\omega't)-i\sin(\omega't)]
\end{align*}
where $\omega'=\sqrt{\omega^2-\lambda^2}$. Again, we define two intermediate
functions $u(t)$ and $v(t)$ as the sum and difference of the solutions, to
separate the sine and cosine terms:
\begin{align*}
  u(t)=x_1(t)+x_2(t) &=2e^{-\lambda t}\cos(\omega't)\\
  v(t)=x_1(t)-x_2(t) &=2ie^{-\lambda t}\sin(\omega't)
\end{align*}
Our final solution is the linear combination of these two intermediate
functions, and we can factor the exponential decay term:
\begin{align}
  \nonumber x(t)&=c_1u(t)+c_2v(t)\\
  \nonumber &=c_1e^{-\lambda t}\cos(\omega't)+c_2e^{-\lambda t}\sin(\omega't)\\
  &=e^{-\lambda t}\left[c_1\cos(\omega't)+c_2\sin(\omega't)\right]
\end{align}
The $c_1\cos(\omega't)+c_2\sin(\omega't)$ term can be combined into a single
cosine or sine term, as we did for the simple harmonic oscillator, and we get
the same solution that we derived in Chapter~\ref{chapter:harmonic-motion}:
\begin{important-equation}
  x(t)= Ae^{-\lambda t}\cos(\omega't+\theta_0)
  \quad\text{where}\quad
  \lambda=\frac b{2m}
  \quad\text{and}\quad
  \omega'=\sqrt{\omega^2-\lambda^2}
  \label{eq:appendix-damped-position}
\end{important-equation}
where $A$ and $\theta_0$ are subject to initial conditions. For the
under-damped oscillator with damping constant $b<\sqrt{4mk}$, the motioon
$x(t)$ has both an exponential decay term and a sinusoidal (oscillatory) term.

For a damped oscillator, the exponential term $e^{-\lambda t}$ in
Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix-damped-position} has a non-zero derivative (slope) at
$t=0$, therefore the phase constant must be non-zero. Here, we have to use both
initial position and velocity to find $A$ and $\theta_0$. Starting with the
initial position $x(0)=A_0$, we can substitute $t=0$ into
Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix-damped-position} to get $A$:
\begin{equation}
  x(0)= A\cancel{e^{-\lambda t}}\cos(\cancel{\omega't}+\theta_0)=A_0
  \quad\quad\longrightarrow\quad\quad
  A=\frac{A_0}{\cos\theta_0}
\end{equation}
We still need to find the phase constant $\theta_0$. Taking the time
derivative (and don't forget to apply the product rule and chain rule
properly!) of Eq.~\ref{eq:appendix-damped-position} gives us the velocity
$v(t)$ of the motion, which is considerably a more complicated expression than
the simple harmonic oscillator:
\begin{equation}
  v(t)=\diff xt
  = -Ae^{-\lambda t}\left[
    \lambda\cos(\omega't+\theta_0)
    +\omega'\sin(\omega't+\theta_0)
    \right]
  \label{eq:appendix-damped-velocity}
\end{equation}
Setting the initial condition $v(0)=0$ at $t=0$, and after bit of algebra and
trigonometry that will be left to you, we find the phase constant $\theta_0$:
\begin{equation}
  v(0) =-A\cancel{e^{-\lambda t}}
  \left[
    \lambda\cos(\cancel{\omega't}+\theta_0)+
    \omega'\sin(\cancel{\omega't}+\theta_0)
    \right]=0
  \quad\longrightarrow\quad
  %0 &=\lambda\cos(\theta_0)+\omega'\sin(\theta_0) \\
  %-\lambda\cos(\theta_0) &= \omega\sin(\theta_0) \\
  %-\frac{\lambda}{\omega'} &=\tan(\theta_0)\\
  \theta_0 =\tan^{-1}\left[-\frac\lambda{\omega'}\right]
\end{equation}
Both $\lambda$ and $\omega'$ can be calculated using the damping factor $b$.
Our final equation for the position for the under-damped oscillator, which is
plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:damping-effects}, is now:
\begin{equation}
  x(t)=\left[\frac{A_0}{\cos\theta_0}\right] e^{-\lambda t}\cos(\omega't+\theta_0)
  \label{eq:underdamped-final}
\end{equation}

\section{Critically-Damped Motion}

At critical damping, where $b=b_c=\sqrt{4mk}$, there is only one (repeating)
real root to the characteristic equation:
\begin{equation}
  r=-\frac{b_c}{2m}=-\lambda_c
\end{equation}
We have at least one solution in the form of
\begin{equation*}
  x_1(t)=e^{-\lambda_ct}
\end{equation*}
But we have already seen from both the (undamped) simple harmonic oscillator
and the under-damped harmonic oscillator, and we will see later in the
over-damped motion, that there should be \emph{two} solutions to the
characteristic equation. Finding the second solution is not as intuitive, but
we note that any solution can be multiplied by a constant. We can extend this
further to \emph{assume} that we may generalize this further to include
functions, i.e.\ the second solution \emph{may} be in the form of:
\begin{equation*}
  x_2=c_2(t)e^{-\lambda_ct}
\end{equation*}
where $c_2(t)$ is a function to be determined, giving us a solution in the form
of:
\begin{equation*}
  x(t)=\left[c_1+u(t)\right]e^{-\lambda_ct}
\end{equation*}
So now all we have to do is to substitute this solution back into the original
ODE and see what (if any) functions $u(t)$ will satisfy the equation.



Finally we find that this function is
\begin{equation*}
  u(t)=t
\end{equation*}


our solution is in the linear combination of the two possible functions, in
the form of:
\begin{important-equation}
  x(t)=(c_1+c_2t)e^{-\lambda_ct}
  \quad\text{where}\quad
  \lambda_c=\frac{b_c}{2m}\quad\text{and}\quad
  b_c=\sqrt{4mk}
\end{important-equation}

Again, we want to find the solution $x(t)$ with motion starting at amplitude
($x(0)=A_0$) from rest (i.e.\ $v(0)=\dot x(0)=0$). Substituting $x(0)=0$ into
the above equation, we can easily find that 
\begin{equation}
  x(0)=(c_1+\cancelto{0}{c_2t})\cancelto{1}{e^{-\lambda_ct}}=A_0
  \quad\to\quad
  c_1=A_0
\end{equation}
Again, we take the derivative of $x$ with respect to time, and using product
rule and chain rule, we find the velocity ($v(t)$) to be:
\begin{equation}
  v(t)=\diff xt=-(c_2\lambda_ct +c_1\lambda_c-c_2)e^{-\lambda_ct}
\end{equation}
putting in initial conditions
\begin{equation}
  v(0)
  =-(\cancelto{0}{c_2\lambda_ct} +c_1\lambda_c-c_2)\cancelto{1}{e^{-\lambda_ct}}
  =0
\end{equation}
which leads us to the equation:
\begin{equation}
  c_1\lambda -c_2=0\quad\to\quad c_2=c_1\lambda=A_0\lambda
\end{equation}
The final solution is
\begin{equation}
  x(t)=A_0(1+\lambda t)e^{-\lambda t}
\end{equation}

\section{Over-Damped Motion}

For the over-damped system, where $b>\sqrt{4mk}$, the solution to the
characteristic equation are real:
\begin{equation*}
  r_1=\frac{-b+\sqrt{b^2-4mk}}{2m}\quad
  r_2=\frac{-b-\sqrt{b^2-4mk}}{2m}
\end{equation*}
We can easily verify that both roots $r_1$ and $r_2$ are real and negative. The
solution $x(t)$ is the linear combination of \emph{two} exponential decay
functions.
\begin{equation}
  x(t)=c_1e^{r_1t}+c_2e^{r_2t}
\end{equation}
where $c_1$ and $c_2$ are based on initial position and velocity.
With initial condition $x(0)=A_0$, we have
\begin{equation}
  x(0)=c_1\cancelto{1}{e^{r_1t}}+c_2\cancelto{1}{e^{r_2t}}=0
  \quad\quad\longrightarrow\quad\quad
  c_1+c_2=A_0
\end{equation}
Taking the derivative with respect to time, we have
\begin{equation}
  v(t)=\diff xt=r_1c_1e^{r_1t}+r_2c_2e^{r_2t}
\end{equation}
With initial condition $v(0)=0$, we have a second equation:
\begin{equation}
  v(0)=r_1c_1+r_2c_2=0
\end{equation}
To find the full solution, we solve the set of linear equations:
\begin{align*}
  c_1 + c_2 &= A_0\\
  c_1r_1 + c_2r_2 &=0
\end{align*}
