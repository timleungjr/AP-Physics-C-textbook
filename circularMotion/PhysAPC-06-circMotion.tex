\chapter{Circular Motion}
\label{chapter:circ-motion}

For the majority of two-dimensional motions (like the ones in
Chapters~\ref{chapter:kinematics} and \ref{chapter:dynamics}) we usually
describe an object's motion using the Cartesian coordinate system. The
position of the object is the $x$- and $y$-coordinates as functions of time:
\begin{equation}
  \bm r(t)=x(t)\iii + y(t)\jjj
\end{equation}
where $\iii$ and $\jjj$ are unit vectors (called basis vectors) that describe
the direction of the $x$ and $y$ axes respectively. We can then take derivatives
with respect to time for each component to get velocity and acceleration.
This type of motion described is called \textbf{rectilinear motion}. However,
the Cartesian coordinate system is not the most intuitive in other types of
motion, called general \textbf{curvilinear motions}. Some examples of
curvilinear motion are shown in Figure~\ref{fig:curvilinear-motions}.

In this chapter, we focus on the simplest form of curvilinear motion, called the
\textbf{circular motion} (Figure~\ref{fig:circ-motion1}), where an object of
mass $m$ moves in a circular path about a fixed centre. We will begin studying
circular motions, first by defining the coordinate system, and then discuss the
kinematics, dynamics and energy conservation.




\section{Polar Coordinate System}

For general curvilinear motions, the \textbf{polar coordinate system} is
preferred over the Cartesian coordinate system. In this case, position of an
object is described by:
\begin{equation}
  \bm r(t)=r(t)\hat{\bm r} + \theta(t)\hat{\bm\theta}
\end{equation}
where $r(t)=|\bm r(t)|$ is distance from the origin, and $\theta(t)$ is the
standard angle, measured counter clockwise from the $x$ axis. Like the
Cartesian system, the polar coordinate system is also right-handed; basis
vectors $\hat{\bm r}$ (``outward radial direction'') and $\hat{\bm\theta}$
(``angular direction'') point in the directions shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:basis-vecs}, and rotate as the object moves. It is clear from
basic geometry that Cartesian and polar coordinates are related by:
\begin{align*}
  x &=r \cos(\theta)\\
  y &=r \sin(\theta)
\end{align*}

\section{Kinematics of Circular Motion}

As is customary for any discussion in mechanics, we begin with the
kinematics---the mathematical description---of circular motion, where the
distance $r$ to the origin is constant.

\subsection{Angular Position}
For a constant radius $r$, the \textbf{angular position} $\theta$ determines an
object's position as a continuous function of
time:
\begin{important-equation}
  \theta=\theta(t)
\end{important-equation}
The unit for angular position is a \emph{radian} (rad). If motion is confined
to the $xy$-plane, then $\theta$ is the standard angle: $\theta$ is positive
when measured counter clockwise from the $x$-axis, and negative when it is
measured clockwise. In the example shown in Figure~\ref{fig:angular-position},

object B is negative. At this time, we are not concerned with what kind of
function $\theta(t)$ is; the exact expression will depend only on the forces

differentiable.

\end{remark}

\subsection{Angular Displacement}

If the object moves along this circular path, then the change in the angular
position is the object's
\textbf{angular displacement}\index{Displacement!Angular}
$\Delta\theta(t)$. It is the difference between an object's current/final
angular position ($\theta(t)$) and its initial angular position ($\theta_0$):
\begin{important-equation}
  \Delta\theta(t)=\theta(t)-\theta_0
  \label{eq:angular-displacement}
\end{important-equation}
Like angular position, $\Delta\theta$ is positive if the object moves in the
counter clockwise direction, and negative if it moves clockwise, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:angular-displacement}. As angular position $\theta(t)$ evolves
in time, so too does angular displacement, therefore $\Delta\theta(t)$ is also a
continuous and continuously differentiable function of time. To absolutely no
one's surprise, angular displacement is also measured in \emph{radians}.

At this point, it should be obvious that the relationship between angular
position and angular displacement is analogous to position and displacement in
one-dimensional kinematics presented in Chapter~\ref{chapter:kinematics}.


\subsection{Angular Velocity and Velocity}

%Analogous to the relationship between position $\bm r$, displacement
%$\Delta\bm r$ and velocity $\bm v$, for circular motion, the
The instantaneous \textbf{angular velocity} $\omega(t)$, also known as
\textbf{angular frequency}, is the time derivative of the angular
position\footnote{Of course,
we can also take the time derivative of angular displacement as well, as
$\theta(t)$ and $\Delta\theta(t)$ only differ by the (constant) initial position
$\theta_0$. And the derivative of a constant is zero.}, measured in
\emph{radian per second} (\si{\radian\per\second}). 
\begin{important-equation}
    \omega(t)=\diff{\theta}t
\end{important-equation}
Angular velocity is also a continuously function of time, but it is not
necessarily continuously differentiable. Using the fundamental theorem of
calculus, angular position $\theta(t)$ and angular displacement
$\Delta\theta(t)$ can be obtained by integrating $\omega(t)$ in time. The
difference between the two integrals, of course, is that the integral for
angular position is indefinite, and the integral for angular displacement is
definite.
\begin{important-equation}
  \theta(t)=\int\omega(t)\dl t +\theta_0
  \quad\quad
  \Delta\theta(t)=\int_{t_0}^t\omega(t)\dl t
\end{important-equation}
If motion is confined to the $xy$-plane, then $\omega$ is positive when the
object moves in the counter clockwise direction, and negative when it moves
clockwise (Figure~\ref{fig:omega-plus-minus}).

As the object moves with angular speed $\omega$, the actual velocity vector
$\bm v$ is tangent to the circle\footnote{For your information, the velocity
vector along \emph{any} path is \emph{always} tangent to the path.}. Obviously
$\bm v$ is not constant in time, as its direction is always changing. However,
there is a simple mathematical relationship between the speed of object
$v(t)=|\bm v(t)|$ and its angular speed $|\omega|$:
\begin{important-equation}
  v=r|\omega|
\end{important-equation}

the angular velocity using this vector cross product:
\begin{important-equation}
  \bm v=\bm\omega\times\bm r
\end{important-equation}
$\bm\omega$ is out of the page if motion is counter clockwise, and into the page
if motion is clockwise. Visualizing $\bm\omega$ takes practice, but this vector
notation is mathematically rigorous and consistent.

\subsection{Angular Acceleration and Tangential Acceleration}
Analogous to the relation between velocity $\bm v$ and acceleration $\bm a$,
wherever $\omega(t)$ is differentiable with time, its time derivative is the
object's \textbf{angular acceleration}:
\begin{important-equation}
  \alpha(t)=\diff{\omega}t=\diff[2]{\theta}t
\end{important-equation}
The unit for angular acceleration is \emph{radian per second squared}
(\si{\radian\per\second\squared}). The sign convention for $\alpha$ is the same
as for $\theta$ and $\omega$: positive for counter clockwise acceleration, and
negative for clockwise acceleration. Similar to the relationship between
velocity and angular velocity, \textbf{tangential acceleration} $a_t$ along the
direction of motion is related to angular acceleration $\alpha$ by the radius
$r$:
\begin{equation}
  a_t(t)=\diff vt=r\diff{\omega}t=r\alpha(t)
\end{equation}
For \emph{uniform} circular motion, $\omega$ is constant, $\alpha=0$, and
therefore $a_t=0$.

By the fundamental theorem of calculus, we can of course integrate angular
acceleration to find the angular velocity, and the \emph{change} in angular
velocity, as functions of time:
\begin{important-equation}
  \omega(t)=\int\alpha(t)\dl t+\omega_0
  \quad\quad
  \Delta\omega(t)=\int_{t_0}^t\alpha(t)\dl t
\end{important-equation}
The relationships are the same as in rectilinear motion.

\subsection{Kinematic Equations}
For constant angular acceleration $\alpha$, the kinematic equations are just
like in rectilinear motion, but with $\theta$ replacing $x$, $\omega$ replacing
$v$, and $\alpha$ replacing $a$:
\begin{align}
  \theta &=\theta_0 + \omega_0 t + \frac12\alpha t^2\\
  \omega &=\omega_0+ \alpha t\\
  \omega^2& = \omega_0^2+ 2\alpha(\theta-\theta_0)
\end{align}
For non-constant $\alpha$, calculus will be required.

\begin{equation}
  \bm a_c=\frac{\Delta\bm v}{\Delta t}=\frac{\bm v_1-\bm v_0}{\Delta t}

\begin{equation}
  |\bm a_c|=\frac{|\Delta\bm v|}{\Delta t}
\end{equation}

That the triangles are similar means that:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{|\Delta\bm r|}r=\frac{|\Delta\bm v|}v
  \quad\rightarrow\quad
  |\Delta\bm v|=\frac vr|\Delta\bm r|
\end{equation}
The magnitude of the centripetal acceleration ($|\bm a_c|$):
\begin{equation}
  |\bm a_c|=\frac{|\Delta\bm v|}{\Delta t}
  =\frac vr\left[\frac{|\Delta\bm r|}{\Delta t}\right]=\frac{v^2}r
\end{equation}
The direction of the centripetal acceleration is easy to show using basic
geometry. When $\Delta t\rightarrow 0$, $\Delta\theta\rightarrow 0$.

Since
\begin{equation}
  2\alpha+\Delta\theta=\ang{180}
\end{equation}
when $\Delta\theta\rightarrow 0$, $\alpha\rightarrow\ang{90}$.

The direction of $\Delta\bm v$ is perpendicular to $\bm v$, Since centripetal
acceleration is in the same direction as $\bm v$, $\bm a_c$ points towards the
centre of the circular path (i.e.\ the inwards radial direction
$-\hat{\bm r}$), giving us the equation for centripetal acceleration, which can
be expressed using the speed $v$ or the angular speed $\omega=v/r$ of the
object:
\begin{important-equation}
  \bm a_c=-\frac{v^2}r\hat{\bm r}=-\omega^2r\hat{\bm r}
  \label{eq:centripetal-acceleration}
\end{important-equation}

In general circular motion, there are two components of acceleration, which are
shown summarized in Figure~\ref{fig:circular-motion-accelerations}:
\begin{itemize}
\item\textbf{Centripetal acceleration} $\bm a_c$ depends on radius of curvature
  $r$ and instantaneous speed $v$. The direction of the acceleration is
  towards the centre of the circular path. Centripetal acceleration is non-zero
  for \emph{all} circular motions.
\item\textbf{Tangential acceleration} $\bm a_t$ depends on radius $r$ and
  angular acceleration $\alpha$. The direction of the acceleration is tangent
  to the circle, along the angular direction $\hat{\bm\theta}$. Tangential
  acceleration is zero for uniform circular motions.
\end{itemize}
The total acceleration is the vector sum of both components:
\begin{important-equation}
  \bm a = \bm a_c+\bm a_t
\end{important-equation}

For circular motion with constant angular velocity $\omega$ (uniform circular
motion), the motion is strictly periodic. The \textbf{frequency} $f$---the
number of revolutions per second---is related to the angular velocity by:
\begin{important-equation}
  f=\frac\omega{2\pi}
\end{important-equation}
The SI unit for frequency in \emph{hertz} (\si\hertz). We can also express
the circular motion using its \textbf{period} ($T$), which is time it takes for
a single revolution along the circular path:
\begin{important-equation}
  T=\frac1f=\frac{2\pi}\omega
\end{important-equation}
The SI unit for period is a \emph{second} (\si\second). Note that frequency and
period are reciprocals of each other.

In uniform circular motion ($\alpha=0$), if the period or frequency are known,
we can express centripetal acceleration in terms of $T$ or $f$:
\begin{important-equation}
  \bm a_c=-\frac{4\pi^2r}{T^2}\hat{\bm r}=-4\pi^2rf^2\hat{\bm r}
\end{important-equation}
\begin{remark}
  Since uniform circular motion is guarenteed to be strictly periodic, there
  will always be a period and a frequency. However, a proper period and
  frequency can still be defined as long as the circular motion is periodic,
  i.e.\ it does not have to be uniform with a constant $\bm\omega$. In such a
  case, the above equation for centripetal acceleration will not be correct.
\end{remark}


\section{Dynamics of Circular Motion}

From the second law of motion $\bm F_\text{net}=m\bm a$, any acceleration must be
caused by a net force along that direction. For circular motions, along the
direction of motion (the angular direction $\hat{\bm\theta}$), the component of
net force that causes tangential cceleration is called the
\textbf{tangential force} $\bm F_t$:
\begin{important-equation}
  \bm F_t=m\bm a_t=mr\alpha\hat{\bm\theta}
\end{important-equation}
In the radial ($\hat{\bm r}$) direction, the component of the net force that
causes centripetal acceleration (directed in the inward radial direction
$-\hat{\bm r}$) is called the \textbf{centripetal force} $\bm F_c$:
\begin{important-equation}
  \bm F_c
  =m\bm a_c
  =-\frac{mv^2}r\hat{\bm r}
  =-m\omega^2r\hat{\bm r}
\end{important-equation}
The forces that generate the centripetal force comes from the free-body
diagram. It can be any of the forces (or a component of that force) that we
have studied in Chapter~\ref{chapter:dynamics}, including, but not limited to:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Gravitational force
\item Kinetic or static friction force
\item Normal force
\item Cable tension force
\item Aerodynamic lift
\end{itemize}

To simplify the analysis, we will ignore forces in the angular direction.
\begin{remark}
  Including tangential forces \emph{greatly} increases the complexity of the
  problem. As a car moves along the track, it experiences a drag force, and
  rolling resistance from the deformation of the tires. Both of these forces
  act in opposition to motion. To maintain speed, a component of the static
  friction must also point in the direction of motion. For front-wheel drive
  car, static friction force points forward, along the direction of
  motion, while in the rear wheels, static friction points backward, opposite
  to motion! Assessing the magnitudes of the actual tangential forces is not a
  simple process, so our analysis will definitely be a simplification.
\end{remark}

The banked curve is an example where the centripetal force is provided by the
horizontal components of two forces: normal force and static friction, between
the tires and the road. Here, we will derive the algebraic expression for the
maximum speed a car can travel along a banked curve.

In contrast, for highway ramps, where the turn radius is high and speed is
comparatively slow, the banking angle is generally less than \ang{10}.

To analyze the banked turn problem, Figure~\ref{fig:banked-turn-forces} shows
the forces acting on a car turns along a banked curve. There are three forces
that acts on it: gravitational force $\bm F_\text{grav}$, normal force
$\bm F_\text{norm}$, and static friction $\bm F_\text{fric}$ between the tires
and the road. For simplicity, we will once again ignore forces in the angular
direction.
\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.1]
    \begin{scope}[rotate=20,thick]
      \node[rotate=20] at (0,0) {
        \pic{.22}{circularMotion/bmw-back-small-shadowy}
      };
      \draw (-3,-1)--+(6,0);
      \draw[rotate around={-20:(-3,-.95)}] (-3,-1)--+(6*cos{20},0)
      node[pos=.25,above=-2]{$\theta$};
    \end{scope}
    \fill[red] circle (.1);
    \begin{scope}[vectors,red]
      \draw (0,0)--(0,-1.7) node[right]{$\bm F_\text{grav}$};
      \draw[rotate=20] (0,0)--(0,1.7*cos{20}) node[above]{$\bm F_\text{norm}$};
      \draw[rotate=20] (0,0)--(-1.5,0) node[left]{$\bm F_\text{fric}$};
    \end{scope}
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Forces on a car when it turns on a banked roadway}
  \label{fig:banked-turn-forces}
\end{figure}

There is no vertical motion, therefore no vertical net force:
\begin{equation}
  \sum F_y=F_\text{norm}\cos\theta-F_\text{fric}\sin\theta-mg=0
  \label{eq:vertical-no-net-force}
\end{equation}
But the horizontal net force is the  centripetal force:
\begin{equation}
  \sum F_x=F_\text{norm}\sin\theta +F_\text{fric}\cos\theta = \frac{mv^2}r
  \label{eq:horizontal-centripetal-force}
\end{equation}
%Friction force $\bm f$ may be static or kinetic.
  





The car turns at maximum speed $v_\text{max}$ when static friction is at its
maximum, i.e.\ when $F_\text{fric}=\mu F_\text{norm}$.
Eqs.\ref{eq:vertical-no-net-force}) and \ref{eq:horizontal-centripetal-force}
become:
\begin{align*}
  F_\text{norm}\left(\cos\theta-\mu\sin\theta\right) &=mg\\
  F_\text{norm}\left(\sin\theta+\mu\cos\theta\right) &=\frac{mv_\text{max}^2}r
\end{align*}
Dividing Eq.~\ref{eq:horizontal-centripetal-force} by
Eq.~\ref{eq:vertical-no-net-force} removes both the normal force and mass terms:
\begin{equation}
  \frac{\sin\theta+\mu\cos\theta}{\cos\theta-\mu\sin\theta}
  =\frac{v_\text{max}^2}{rg}
\end{equation}
The \emph{maximum} velocity $v_\text{max}$ at which a car can turn on a banked
curve is:
\begin{important-equation}
  \text{Banked road:}\quad
  v_\text{max}=
    \sqrt{rg\frac{\sin\theta+\mu\cos\theta}{\cos\theta-\mu\sin\theta}}
\end{important-equation}
Note that $v_\text{max}$ does not depend on mass of the car.

For a flat roadway with no banking ($\theta=0$), therefore $\sin\theta=0$ and
$\cos\theta=1$, the equation reduces to:
\begin{important-equation}
  \text{Level road:}\quad v_{\text{max}}=\sqrt{\mu rg}
\end{important-equation}
When there is no friction (i.e.\ $\mu=0$), the speed that the car can travel
reduces to:
\begin{important-equation}
  \text{Banked road with no friction:}\quad v=\sqrt{rg\tan\theta}
\end{important-equation}
In the frictionless case, the speed is no longer a ``maximum''. The car can
only travel in \emph{one} speed without deviating from the track. Travelling to
quickly would result in the car sliding off the outside of the road; while
travelling too slowly would result in the car sliding towards the centre of
the path.

total energy of the system is constant, i.e.:
\begin{equation*}

\end{equation*}

find the tension force on the pendulum, nor the acceleration of the pendulum

is a tangential acceleration with a magnitude of:
\begin{equation}
  a_t=\frac{F_t}m=g\sin\theta_\text{max}
\end{equation}
This is the same acceleration as an object sliding down a frictionless ramp at
an angle of $\theta_\text{max}$. As the pendulum returns to the bottom of the
swing, tangential acceleration will decrease to zero.

At the bottom of the swing, where $\theta=0$, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:bottom-swing},
\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.8]
    \fill[pattern=north east lines] (-1,0) rectangle (1,.2);
    \draw[very thick] (-1,0)--(1,0);
    \draw[thick] (0,0)--(0,-5);
    \shade[ball color=red] (0,-5) circle (0.2) node[below right]{$m$};
    \draw[vector,red] (0,-5)--(0,-2.5) node[right]{$\bm F_\text{ten}$};
    \draw[vector,red] (0,-5)--(0,-6.5) node[below]{$\bm F_\text{grav}$};
    \draw[dashed] (3.54,-3.54) arc (315:225:5);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{A simple pendulum at the bottom of its swing}
  \label{fig:bottom-swing}
\end{figure}
we have already shown from the law of conservation of energy that the velocity
must be at its maximum value\footnote{We have not shown the work leading to
this final expression; this is already done in Chapter~\ref{chapter:energy}}:
\begin{equation}
  v=\sqrt{2g\ell(1-\cos\theta_\text{max})}
\end{equation}
therefore centripetal acceleration and centripetal force are at maximum
magnitude:
\begin{align*}
  a_c &=\frac{v^2}\ell=2g(1-\cos\theta_\text{max})\\
  F_c &= ma_c=2mg(1-\cos\theta_\text{max})
\end{align*}
Using the second law of motion along the radial direction, we can show that, at
the lowest point, tension must be at the highest:
\begin{align*}
  F_\text{ten} &= F_\text{grav}+F_c\\
  &= mg+2mg(1-\cos\theta_\text{max})\\
  &= mg(3-2\cos\theta_\text{max})
\end{align*}
There is no tangential acceleration because there are forces in the angular
direction:
\begin{equation*}
  F_t=0\quad\longrightarrow\quad a_t=0
\end{equation*}



\begin{example}[Yo-Yo]
  \label{example:yoyo}
  You are playing with a yo-yo with a mass $m$; the length of the string is
  $R$. You decide to see how slowly you can swing it in a vertical circle while
  keeping the string taut, even when the yo-yo is at the top of its swing.
  Assuming that there is no friction, drag or other damping forces:
  \begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=18pt,itemsep=6pt]
  \item Calculate the minimum speed at which you can swing the yo-yo while
    keeping it on a circular path.
  \item Given that the yo-yo is at its minimum speed at the top of its swing,
    express the tension force as a function of the angle deflection from the
    vertical ($\theta$) using given quantities and fundamental constants.
  %\item If the yo-yo is at its minimum speed at the top of its swing, find the
  %  tension in the string when the yo-yo is at the side and at the bottom.
  \end{enumerate}
 
  \vspace{.1in}\textbf{Solution:} This is a very typical problem for vertical
  motion. To solve it, we first draw free-body diagram for when the yo-yo is
  at the top of the circle. Without friction, drag or other damping forces, two
  forces act on the yo-yo: gravitational force ($\bm F_\text{grav}=m\bm g$)
  pointing downwards, and tension force ($\bm F_\text{ten}$) pointing along the
  string.
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}
      \draw circle (2);
      \fill[red] (0,2) circle (.13);
      \draw[vector,red] (-.06,2)--+(0,-1) node[left]{$\bm F_\text{ten}$};
      \draw[vector,red] (.06,2)--+(0,-1.5) node[right]{$\bm F_\text{grav}$};
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  At the top of the circle, centripetal force is provided by \emph{both}
  gravitational and tension forces, as both forces point downwards. Since $m$,
  $g$ and $R$ are all constants, minimum velocity $v=v_\text{min}$ on the right
  hand side of the equation must occur when $F_\text{ten}=0$ on the left side.
  We are left with:
  \begin{displaymath}
    F_c = ma_c\quad\rightarrow\quad
    F_\text{ten}+mg = \frac{mv^2}R\quad\rightarrow\quad
    mg = \frac{mv_\text{min}^2}R
  \end{displaymath}
  Cancelling mass on both side of the equation, and solving for $v_\text{min}$,
  we find the minimum speed at the top of the swing to be:
  \begin{displaymath}
    v^2=gR\quad\rightarrow\quad\boxed{v_\text{min} = \sqrt{gR}}
  \end{displaymath}
  This minimum speed is independent of the mass of the yo-yo. This should not be
  a surprise from the conservation of energy problems that we have solved for
  the simple pendulum.

  A vertical circular motion such as this one is not uniform (i.e.\ the speed of
  the yo-yo is not constant), therefore we must also use conservation of energy
  to solve the rest of the question. After the yo-yo has rotated from the top
  by an angle $\theta$:
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.1]
      \draw[<->] (0,0)--(0,2) node[pos=.4,left]{$R$};
      \foreach \y in {0,2} \draw (-2.5,\y)--+(3.5,0);
      \draw (-2.5,2*sin{35})--+(3.5,0);% node[pos=0,left]{$h=0$};
      \draw circle (2);
      \begin{scope}[red,rotate=55]
        \draw (0,0)--(0,2);
        \fill (0,2) circle (.06);
        \draw[vectors] (0,2)--+(0,-1.5) node[below=0]{$\vec F_\text{ten}$};
        \draw[vectors,rotate around={-55:(0,2)}]
        (0,2)--+(0,-1.5) node[below=0]{$\vec F_\text{grave}$};
      \end{scope}
      \draw[<->] (.5,0)--(.5,2*sin{35}) node[midway,right]{$R\cos\theta$};
      \draw[<->] (.5,2*sin{35})--(.5,2) node[midway,right]{$R-R\cos\theta$};
      \draw[red,thick] (0,2) circle (.06);
      \draw[axes] (0,2) arc (90:145:2) node[midway,below]{$\theta$};
      \fill circle (.05);
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  It's height is lowered by
  \begin{displaymath}
    R-R\cos\theta=R(1-\cos\theta)
  \end{displaymath}
  Using the same conservation of energy method used for the simple pendulum,
  which was discussed extensively in Chapter~\ref{chapter:energy}, we can find
  the speed $v$ as a function of $\theta$:
  \begin{align*}
    \Delta K + \Delta U_\text{grav} &=0 \\
    \left[\frac12mv^2-\frac12mv_\text{min}^2\right]-mgR(1-\cos\theta) &=0
  \end{align*}
  We can, once again, cancel the mass term, and solve for $v^2$, we get
  \begin{displaymath}
    v^2=gR(3-2\cos\theta)
  \end{displaymath}
  Now that we have the speed of the yo-yo at any angle of deflection, we can
  return to the equations for centripetal force to calculate the tension. In
  this case, the centripetal force is provided by tension force, and
  \emph{the component of gravity directed towards the centre}, i.e.:
  %    \node[text width=11cm,fill=red!10] (fc) at (6.7,2.1){

%
  \begin{displaymath}
    F_c = ma_c\quad\rightarrow\quad
    F_\text{ten} + F_\text{grav}\cos\theta = \frac{mv^2}R
  \end{displaymath}
  Since we have already calculated the square of the speed $v^2$ as a function
  of $\theta$ using conservation of energy, we can directly substitute that
  expression into the above equation. Solving for $F_\text{ten}$, we have:
%      But we do not know the speed $v_\text{side}$ yet.

%        K_\text{top} + U_\text{top} = K_\text{side}\quad\to\quad
