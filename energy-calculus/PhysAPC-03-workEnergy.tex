\chapter{Work and Energy}
\label{chapter:energy}

The topic of work and energy is, perhaps, the most important topic in
fundamental physics. It is also a concept that is most
misunderstood\footnote{And in the opinion of the author, often badly taught as
a result.}, as especially in the area of conservation of energy, and so it is
often presented in a way that is confusing to students at many levels. In this
document, we aim to approach this topic from a logical perspective that
combines knowledge in kinematics as well as dynamics.

We start with some definition at are (unfortunately) not very useful:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item \textbf{Energy} is the ability to do work.
\item \textbf{Work} is the mechanism in which energy is transformed.
\end{itemize}
At a minimum, these definitions at least tell two things:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item The concepts of work and energy cannot be separated: Defining work
  without leading to energy is rather pointless; defining energy without first
  referring to work makes no sense.
\item Word definitions are not enough. We also need to have \emph{mathematical}
  definitions as well.
\end{itemize}
It is customary to start with the definition of mechanical work, and use it
to define different forms of energies.




\section{Mechanical Work}
\label{sec:mechwork}
An infinitesimal amount of \textbf{mechanical work} $\dl W$ is done when a
force $\bm F$ displaces an object by an infinitesimal amount $\dl{\bm r}$. If
the force moves an object along the path $\mathcal C$, the total work $W$ done
by the force is defined by the integral:
\begin{important-equation}
  W=\int_{\mathcal C}\dl W=\int_{\mathcal C}\bm F(\bm r)\cdot\dl{\bm r}
  \label{work-definition}
\end{important-equation}
While both force and displacement are vectors, work is a scalar quantity. Work
by $\bm F$ can be positive or negative depending on the dot product. In
general, the amount of work done depends on the path $\mathcal C$. No work is
done if the force is perpendicular to displacement, (i.e.\
$\bm F\cdot\dl{\bm r}=0$) which means that the force did not \emph{cause}
the displacement, or if the object does not move, (i.e.\
$\dl{\bm r}=\bm 0$), or if no force is applied during motion (i.e.\
$\bm F=\bm 0$).

For motion confined to one direction along a one-dimensional coordinate system,
Eq.~\ref{work-definition} reduces to\footnote{Note that direction still matters
for $F$ and $x$, even in 1D, in that there is still a positive and negative
direction (if $F$ and $\dl x$ are in the same direction, then $W>0$; and if $F$
and $\dl x$ are in opposite direction, then $W<0$).}:
\begin{important-equation}
  W=\int_{x_0}^{x_1} F(x)\dl x
\end{important-equation}
For a constant force that moves an object along a straight path, the integral
simplifies to just the dot product of two vectors\endnote{We must remember how
  to express the dot products. If you know the angles between the vectors,
  then:
  \begin{equation*}
    \bm A\cdot\bm B= AB\cos\theta
  \end{equation*}
  And if you know the components of the vectors, then
  \begin{equation*}
    \bm A\cdot\bm B= A_xB_x+A_yB_y+A_zB_z
  \end{equation*}
}:
\begin{important-equation}
  W = \bm F\cdot\Delta\bm r=F\Delta r\cos\theta
  \label{eq:no-integration}
\end{important-equation}
where $\theta$ is the angle between the force and displacement vectors. We can
also use the above equation if the force $\bm F$ is \emph{averaged} over
the displacement, also known as a \textbf{spatial average}:
\begin{equation}
  \bm F=\bm F_\text{avg}=\frac{\int_{x_0}^{x_1} F(x)\dl x}{x_1-x_0}
\end{equation}
\begin{remark}
  At this time, we need a short discussion about what this \emph{average} is.
  In this case, the force is averaged over the \emph{displacement} that the
  object travels. This is called a \emph{spatial average}. We are not concerned
  about how long it takes for the object to move. In contrast, when computing
  the \emph{impulse} generated from an average force over a \emph{time}
  interval, which is related to the change in the \emph{momentum} of an object,
  the force is averaged over \emph{time}. This is called a \emph{time average}:
  \begin{displaymath}
    \overline{\bm F}=\frac{\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \bm F(t)\dl t}{t_1-t_0}
  \end{displaymath}
  Here, we purposely use different notation to distinguish different types of
  averaging. Time averaging does not consider the actual motion of the object.
  This time averaging is also how we get the expressions for average velocity
  and average acceleration in Chapter~\ref{chapter:kinematics}, when we
  describe the motion of a single object:
  \begin{displaymath}
    \overline{\bm v}=\frac{\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \bm v(t)\dl t}{t_1-t_0}
    \quad\text{and}\quad
    \overline{\bm a}=\frac{\int_{t_0}^{t_1} \bm a(t)\dl t}{t_1-t_0}
  \end{displaymath}
  Later in this chapter, we will also discuss the concept of average power,
  which uses a time averaging.

  However, there is still a third kind of averaging that we will briefly
  mention here. In the thermodynamics of gases, when we calculate the average
  speed and kinetic energies of many gas molecules, the average is the
  arithmetic mean of all the speeds:
  \begin{displaymath}
    \langle v\rangle = \frac{\sum v_i}N
  \end{displaymath}
  And if the speeds are given in a probability density function $f(v)$,
  \begin{displaymath}
    \langle v\rangle = \int_0^\infty vf(v)\dl v
  \end{displaymath}
  This type of averaging is called an \textbf{ensemble average}. While it is
  crucial to know the difference between different types of averaging, when
  solving problems, it is usually very clear from the context. 
\end{remark}

At this moment, we have not specified what the force $\bm F$ is. Whereas we
always use the \emph{net force} when calculating acceleration in dynamics
problems, when calculating the work done by ``a force'', it could mean:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=6pt]
\item\textbf{Work done by a \emph{specific} force.} As you have no doubt seen
  in dynamics problems, there are usually multiple forces acting on an object.
  We can calculate, based on the motion of the object, how much work is done by
  each force. Calculating the work done by a specific force allows us to study
  the \emph{exact} mechanism in how energy is transformed.
  
\item\textbf{Work done by the \emph{net} force}, in other words, the \emph{sum}
  of all the work done by each force. This is also called the \textbf{net work}
  $W_\text{net}$:
  \begin{equation*}
    W_\text{net}
    = \sum_i W_i
    =\int_{\mathcal C}\bm F_\text{net}(\bm r)\cdot\dl{\bm r}
  \end{equation*}
  The total work on an an object allows us to examine how the motion of an
  object changes.
  %The net work allows us to study what is the overall conservation of energy
  %to the entire object.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{example}
  A worker pushes a heavy crate up a ramp of angle $\phi$ with a varying
  applied force. The free-body diagram for this problem is shown below. Here,
  four forces act on the crate: gravitational force $\bm F_\text{grav}$, normal
  force $\bm F_\text{norm}$, kinetic friction $\bm F_\text{fric}$, and applied
  force $\bm F_\text{app}(x)$, which changes in magnitude as the crate's moves.
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.85,thick]
      \draw (0,0)--(5,0);
      \draw[axes] (3,0) arc (0:25:3) node[midway,right]{$\phi$};
      \begin{scope}[rotate=25]
        \draw (0,0)--({5/cos(25)},0);
        \draw[|->] (3.5,1.75)--+(2,0) node[right]{$\Delta x$};
        \draw[mass] (1.5,0) rectangle (3.5,1.5);
        \fill[red] (2.5,.75) circle (.07);
        \begin{scope}[vector,red]
          \draw (2.5,.75)--+(1.5,0) node[right]{$\bm F_\text{app}(x)$};
          \draw (2.5,.75)--+(0,1.5) node[above]{$\bm F_\text{norm}$};
          \draw[rotate around={-25:(2.5,.75)}] (2.5,.75)--+(0,-1.25)
          node[below]{$\bm F_\text{grav}$};
          \draw (2.5,.75)--+(-1,0) node[left]{$\bm F_\text{fric}$};
        \end{scope}
      \end{scope}
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  We can calculate the work done by each force:
  \begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
  \item Work done by the normal force is zero, because $\bm F_\text{norm}$ is
    perpendicular to the direction of motion:
    \begin{equation*}
      W_\text{norm}=\int\underbracket[1pt]{\bm F_\text{norm}\cdot\dl{\bm r}}_{=0}=0
    \end{equation*}
  \item Work done by kinetic friction is negative, because $\bm F_\text{fric}$
    is in the opposite direction to motion. Since kinetic friction is constant
    in this case, we can avoid integration:
    \begin{equation*}
      W_\text{fric}=\int\bm F_\text{fric}\cdot\dl{\bm r}
      =-\int F_\text{fric}\dl x=-\mu_k F_\text{norm}\Delta x<0
    \end{equation*}
  \item Work done by gravitational force is also negative, because the
    component of gravity along the direction of motion is in the opposite
    direction. Gravitational force is also a constant force, and integration is
    not necessary:
    \begin{equation*}
      W_\text{grav}=\int\bm F_\text{grav}\cdot\dl{\bm r}
      =\int F_\text{grav}\cos(\phi+\SI{90}\deg)\dl x
      =mg\Delta x\cos(\phi+\SI{90}\deg)<0
    \end{equation*}
  \item Finally, the work done by the applied force is positive, because
    applied force is in the same direction as motion.
    \begin{equation*}
      W_a(x)=\int_{x_0}^x F_\text{app}\dl x>0
    \end{equation*}
  \end{itemize}
  The total work (i.e.\ the net work) done by summing the work done by each
  force:
  \begin{align*}
    W_\text{net} &=W_a+W_\text{norm}+W_\text{grav} +W_\text{fric}\\
    &=\int\underbracket[1pt]{
      \left(\bm F_\text{app}+\bm F_\text{norm}+\bm F_\text{grav}+\bm F_\text{fric}\right)
    }_{\bm F_\text{net}}\cdot\dl{\bm r} =\int\bm F_\text{net}\cdot\dl{\bm r}
  \end{align*}
\end{example}



\section{Definition of Kinetic Energy}
\label{sec:transKE}
When a net force acts on an object to accelerate it, we can calculate the
consequence of the work done by the net force. For now, we will assume that the
object has constant mass. For motion in one direction along a one-dimensional
coordinate system, the total/net work done is given by:
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{net}
  =\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F_\text{net}(x)\dl x
  =\int ma\dl x
  =m\int\diff vt\dl x
\end{equation*}
Since both $v(t)$ and $x(t)$ are continuously differentiable in time, we can
switch the order of the differentiation:
\begin{equation*}
  =m\int\diff xt\dl v=m\int_{v_0}^{v_1}v\dl v
\end{equation*}
The limits of integration switch from the initial and final position ($x_0$ and
$x_1$) to the initial and final velocities, where $v_0=v(x_0)$ and
$v_1=v(x_1)$. Evaluating this integral, we have:
\begin{equation*}
  =m\int_{v_0}^{v_1}v\dl v
  =\frac12mv^2\Big|^{v_1}_{v_0}
  =\frac12mv_1^2-\frac12mv_0^2
  =\Delta K
\end{equation*}
where $K$ is defined as the object's \textbf{kinetic energy}:
\begin{important-equation}
  K=\frac12mv^2
  \label{eq:KE-definition}
\end{important-equation}
\begin{remark}
  $K$ is more specifically called the \textbf{translational kinetic energy},
  and must be distinguished from the \textbf{rotational kinetic energy} which
  is used when an object is rotating about a pivot. The topic of rotational
  motion and rotational kinetic energy is discussed in detail in
  Chapter~\ref{chapter:rotMotion}.
\end{remark}
The very \emph{definition} of kinetic energy came from this integration: when
the net force on an object is doing work, that work is equal to the change in
some quantity, and we \emph{define} that quantity as the kinetic energy. This
is known as the \textbf{work-energy principle}:
\begin{important-equation}
  W_\text{net}=\Delta K
  \label{work-energy-theorem}
\end{important-equation}
When multiple forces act on an object, \emph{positive} net work will
\emph{increase} the kinetic energy of the object by the same amount, while
\emph{negative} net work will decreases kinetic energy by the same amount.
Eq.~\ref{work-energy-theorem} applies regardless of \emph{what} the net force
is comprised of.

\textbf{Crucially, the work-energy principle turns a generally difficult vector
integration problem (integrating $W_\text{net}$ on the left hand side) into a
simple scalar algebraic expression ($\Delta K$ on the right hand side). As we
will see later in the chapter, this change from a vector calculus problem to a
scalar algebra problem has profound implications in our ability to solve
motion problems quickly.}
\begin{remark}
  When you integrate the net work done ``properly'' in two or three dimensions,
  the only difference is the dot product, which only \emph{slightly} increases
  the complexity of the problem compared to the 1D case:
  \begin{equation*}
    W_\text{net}
    = \int\bm F_\text{net}(\bm r)\cdot\dl{\bm r}
    = \cdots = m\int_{\bm v_0}^{\bm v_1}\bm v\cdot\dl{\bm v}
  \end{equation*}
  The dot product is a lot easier to evaluate than you might think (albeit it
  makes our algebra more tedious)
  \begin{equation*}
    \bm v\cdot\dl{\bm v}=v_x\dl v_x+v_y\dl v_y+v_z\dl v_z
  \end{equation*}
  In this case, we have to add up the work done in all three dimensions of
  motion:
  \begin{align*}
    m\int\bm v\cdot\dl{\bm v}
    &=m\left(
    \int_{v_{x0}}^{v_{x1}} v_x\dl v_x +
    \int_{v_{y0}}^{v_{y1}} v_y\dl v_y +
    \int_{v_{z0}}^{v_{z1}} v_z\dl v_z\right)\\
    &=\left(\frac12mv_{x1}^2+\frac12mv_{y1}^2 + \frac12mv_{z1}^2\right)-
    \left(\frac12mv_{x0}^2+\frac12mv_{y0}^2 + \frac12mv_{z0}^2\right)
  \end{align*}
  Noting that $v^2=v_x^2+v_y^2+v_z^2$, the result of this integrating is the same
  as in the one-dimensional case:
  \begin{displaymath}
    W_\text{net}=\frac12mv_1^2-\frac12mv_0^2=\Delta K
  \end{displaymath}
\end{remark}
Even though we are able to equate the net work done on an object to the change
in kinetic energy in 1D, 2D and 3D cases, it should be noted that
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item In a one-dimensional coordinate system, the net force on an object must
  be either along the direction of motion, or opposite to motion. Therefore net
  force would \emph{always} do work, and there would always be a change in
  energy.
\item However, in two or higher dimensions, the net force can be perpendicular
  to motion. Therefore the presence of a net force does \emph{not} mean that the
  net force is necessarily doing work. A good example is in circular motion
  (Chapter~\ref{chapter:circ-motion}), where the net force towards the centre of
  the circular path, called the centripetal force, can never do any work.
\end{itemize}


\begin{example}[A basic integration problem]
  A net force $F(x)=4x$ where $F$ is measured in newtons, and $x$ is measured in
  metres, acts on an object of mass \SI2{\kilo\gram} as it moves along the
  $x$-axis from $x_0=1$ to $x_1=\SI5\metre$. Given that the object is initially
  at rest at $x_0$, What is the net work done on this object? And what is its
  final speed?

  \textbf{Solution:} Since net force is not constant, we have to integrate from
  $x_0$ to $x_1$ to calculate the work done:
  \begin{displaymath}
    W_\text{net}=
    \int_{x_0}^{x_1} F_\text{net}(x)\dl x=
    \int_1^5 4x\dl x= 4\left[\frac{x^2}2\right]^5_1=\boxed{\SI{48}\joule}
  \end{displaymath}
  From the work-energy principle, the net work done is equal to the change in
  the object's kinetic energy. The object is initially at rest, therefore
  $K_0=0$, and the calculations becomes:
  \begin{displaymath}
    W_\text{net}=\Delta K=K_1-\cancel{K_0}=\frac12mv_1^2
    \quad\longrightarrow\quad
    v_1^2=\sqrt{\frac{2W_\text{net}}m}=
    \sqrt{\frac{2(48)}2}=\boxed{\SI{6.9}{\metre\per\second}}
  \end{displaymath}
\end{example}



\section{Potential Energy}

Unlike kinetic energy, forms of energy that can be stored are called
\textbf{potential energy}.

\subsection{Gravitational Potential Energy}
\label{section:gravitationalPE}

Consider an object that is free-falling under the gravitational force,
through a displacement of $\Delta\bm r$, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:falling1}.
\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.6]
    \draw[thick] (-4,0)--+(6,0) node[midway,below]{\small Earth}
    node[right]{$h=0$};
    
    \draw[dashed] (-4,6)--+(6,0);
    \draw[dashed] (-4,3)--+(6,0);
    
    \draw[mass] (0,6) circle (.2) node[above=3]{$m$};
    \draw[vector,red] (0,6)--+(0,-2) node[right]{$\bm F_\text{grav}$};
    \draw[vector] (-.5,6)--+(0,-3) node[midway,left]{$\Delta\bm r$};
    \draw[vector,blue] (-1.7,0)--+(0,6) node[midway,left]{$h_0$};
    \draw[vector,blue] (-1,0)--+(0,3) node[midway,right]{$h_1$};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Gravitational force doing positive work on a free-falling object.}
  \label{fig:falling1}
\end{figure}
\begin{remark}
  Figure~\ref{fig:falling1} shows an object that is falling downwards, however,
  we a free-falling object can also be moving upwards or sideways, as long as
  gravitational force is the only force acting on it, i.e.\
  $\bm F_\text{grav}=\bm F_\text{net}$.
\end{remark}
We have specifically chosen a free fall because we want to isolate the work
done by gravitational force alone, without worrying about any other forces.
Since gravitational force is the net force, the work done by gravitational
force is also the net work on the object, i.e.\ $W_\text{grav}=W_\text{net}$.

When the displacement $\Delta\bm r$ is small, acceleration due to gravity
$\bm g$ can be considered to be constant, therefore $\bm F_\text{grav}=m\bm g$
is a constant force. Work done by gravitational force can be calculated without
using any integration.

If the object falls downwards, i.e.\ both $\bm F_\text{grav}$ and $\Delta\bm r$
are in the same direction, work done by gravitational force ($W_\text{grav}$) is
positive. From the work-energy principle (Eq.~\ref{work-energy-theorem}),
there must be an increase in kinetic energy \emph{by the same amount as work},
and the object speeds up:
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{grav}=W_\text{net}=mg\Delta r>0
  \quad\longrightarrow\quad
  \Delta K = W_\text{grav} > 0
\end{equation*}
Conversely, if the object moves upwards, i.e.\ $\bm F_\text{grav}$ and
$\Delta\bm r$ are in opposite direction, then work done by gravitational force
is negative. There would now be a decrease in kinetic energy
\emph{by the same amount as work}, and the object slows down.
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{grav}=W_\text{net}=mg\Delta r<0
  \quad\longrightarrow\quad
  \Delta K = W_\text{grav} < 0
\end{equation*}
The change in kinetic energy must be, of course, consistent with kinematics and
dynamics, since the expression for kinetic energy was derived from the second
law of motion and kinematic equations.

But the work done by gravitational force can also be expressed in terms of the
change in the object's height. Using the ground as the \textbf{reference}
(this is where $h=0$ and $U_\text{grav}=0$), we can express the displacement
$\Delta r$ in terms of the initial height $h_0$ and final height $h_1$. The
work done by gravitational force can therefore be written as:
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{grav}= mg(h_0-h_1)
\end{equation*}
The term $h_0-h_1$ is rather curious, because when there is a change in a
physical quantity, we usually express the difference as subtracting the initial
quantity from the final quantity. For example, displacement is defined as
$\Delta\bm r=\bm r_1-\bm r_0$, change in velocity is
$\Delta\bm v=\bm v_1-\bm v_0$, and even earlier in Section~\ref{sec:transKE},
we defined the change in kinetic energy as $\Delta K=K_1-K_0$. It's always final
minus initial. To ``fix'' this inconsistency to get a $h_1-h_0$ term in this
case,  we can
%get the terms in their proper order by
factor out a $-1$: % factor:
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{grav} = mg(h_0-h_1)=-mg(h_1-h_0)
\end{equation*}
Multiplying the $mg$ terms back inside the bracket, we can relate the work done
by gravitational force to the change of a new motion quantity $U_\text{grav}$:
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{grav} = -(mgh_1-mgh_0) = -\Delta U_\text{grav}
\end{equation*}
where $U_\text{grav}$ is the \textbf{gravitational potential energy}, defined as:
\begin{important-equation}
  U_\text{grav}=mgh
  \label{eq:easy-Ug}
\end{important-equation}
In defining gravitational potential energy, we have explicitly used the
\emph{ground} as our reference, where $U_\text{grav}=0$. But it should be noted
that \textbf{the choice of the reference is arbitrary}; whichever simplifies
the problem solving process can be used as the reference, as long as the
reference is consistent throughout the problem-solving process. What reference
we choose does not pose any mathematical issues because we are ultimately
interested in the \emph{change} in gravitational potential energy, which is
related to the work done by gravity:
\begin{important-equation}
  W_\text{grav} =-\Delta U_\text{grav}
  \quad\text{where}\quad
  \Delta U_\text{grav}=mg\Delta h
  \label{work-potential-energy}
\end{important-equation}


As mentioned before, when an object is in the free fall, the work done by
gravitational force \emph{is} the net work. Therefore we can equate the change
in kinetic energy to change in gravitational potential energy:
\begin{align*}
  W_\text{grav}&=W_\text{net}\\
  -\Delta U_\text{grav} &= \Delta K
\end{align*}
What work by gravitational force is to exchange energy between gravitational
potential energy and kinetic energy.

%In Eq.~\ref{work-potential-energy}, we note
We can now summarize some special properties in the relationships between the
work done by gravity ($W_\text{grav}$) and gravitational potential energy
($U_\text{grav}$):

\begin{definition}
  \begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
  \item When work done by gravity \emph{positive}, (i.e.\ $W_\text{grav}>0$),
    there is a \emph{decrease} in gravitational potential energy (i.e.\
    $U_\text{grav}<0$) by the same amount;
  \item When work done by gravity is \emph{negative}, (i.e.\ $W_\text{grav}<0$),
    there is a \emph{increase} in gravitational potential energy (i.e.\
    $U_\text{grav}>0$) by the same amount;
  \item Positive work by gravity transforms gravitational potential energy into
    kinetic energy; negative work by gravity transforms kinetic energy into
    gravitational potential energy;
  \item Work by the gravitational force is \emph{path independent}:
    $W_\text{grav}$ depends on the end points $h_0$ and $h_1$, but not
    \emph{how} the object moves from $h_0$ to $h_1$
  \item Only work done by gravitational force $\bm F_\text{grav}$ can affect
    gravitational potential energy $U_\text{grav}$
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}
If the displacement $\Delta\bm r$ is sufficiently large that we can no longer
treat $\bm g$ as a constant, we will have to use the law of universal
gravitation between two point masses to express the gravitational force:
\begin{equation*}
  F_\text{grav}=\frac{Gm_1m_2}{r^2}
\end{equation*}
In this case, the expression for gravitational potential energy is obtained by
integrating the law of universal gravitation, from $r_0$ to $r_1$:
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{grav}
  =\int_{r_0}^{r_1} F_\text{grav}\dl r
  = Gm_1m_2\int_{r_0}^{r_1}\frac{\dl r}{r^2}
  =-\frac{Gm_1m_2} r\Big|^{r_2}_{r_1}=-\Delta U_\text{grav}
\end{equation*}
giving us the general equation for gravitational potential energy
\begin{equation*}
  U_\text{grav}=-\frac{Gm_1m_2}r
\end{equation*}
The topic of universal gravitation is discussed further in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:gravity}.

\subsection{Spring Force \& Elastic Potential Energy}
The \textbf{spring force}, or \textbf{elastic force} $\bm F_\text{elas}$, is
the force that a compressed/stretched spring exerts on the object connected to
it. As we discussed in Chapter~\ref{chapter:dynamics}, an \emph{ideal} spring
obeys \textbf{Hooke's law}, which states that the spring force is proportional
to the amount of spring displacement $\bm x$, and acts in the opposition to the
displacement vector, as shown in Figure~\ref{hooke1}.
\begin{equation*}
  \bm F_\text{elas}=-k\bm x
\end{equation*}
The constant $k$ that relates the spring force and spring displacement is
called the \textbf{spring constant}, with a unit of \emph{newton per metre}
(\si{\newton\per\metre}). The spring constant represents the stiffness of the
spring, and it depends on the size and geometry of the spring, as well as the
material that it is made of.

Following the analysis that we did for gravitational force, we set up a

We can clearly see that the net force on the mass must come entirely from the
spring force, i.e. $\bm F_\text{net}=\bm F_\text{elas}$. Therefore the net work
done on the mass must also be equal to the work done by the spring:
$W_\text{net}=W_\text{elas}$. This system is designed so that we can isolate the
work done by the spring alone without influence of other forces.

If the mass moves in the same direction as the spring force,, work done by
the spring ($W_\text{grav}$) is positive. From the work-energy principle
(Eq.~\ref{work-energy-theorem}), there must be an increase in kinetic energy
\emph{by the same amount as work}, and the object speeds up:
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{elas}=W_\text{net}=\Delta K > 0
\end{equation*}
Conversely, if the mass moves in the opposite direction to the spring force,
then work done by the spring is negative. There would now be a decrease in
kinetic energy \emph{by the same amount as work}, and the object slows down:
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{elas}=W_\text{net}=\Delta K = < 0
\end{equation*}
As was in the case in gravity, the change in kinetic energy is consistent with
kinematics and dynamics. An in-depth discussion is presented later in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:harmonic-motion}, on harmonic motion. As the spring force
moves the mass, the work done by the spring force on the object is:
\begin{equation}
  W_\text{elas}
  =\int\bm F_\text{elas}\cdot\dl{\bm r}
  =\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F_\text{elas}\dl x
  =-k\int_{x_0}^{x_1} x\dl x
  =-\left(\frac12kx_1^2-\frac12kx_0^2\right)=-\Delta U_\text{elas}
  \label{eq:spring-pot1}
\end{equation}
where $U_\text{elas}$ is defined as the \textbf{elastic potential energy}:
\begin{important-equation}
  U_\text{elas}=\frac12kx^2
\end{important-equation}
Crucially, the work done by the spring force is related to the elastic
potential energy by:
\begin{important-equation}
  W_\text{elas}=-\Delta U_\text{elas}
\end{important-equation}
As mentioned before, when a mass in this horizontal spring-mass system is
moving under the sole influence of the spring force, the work done by the
spring \emph{is} the net work. Therefore we can equate the change in kinetic
energy to change in elastic potential energy:
\begin{equation*}
  W_\text{elas}=W_\text{net}\quad\longrightarrow\quad
  -\Delta U_\text{elas} = \Delta K
\end{equation*}
Work by the spring force exchanges energy between elastic potential energy
and kinetic energy. With that, we an now summarize some special properties in
the relationships between the work done by the spring force ($W_\text{elas}$)
and elastic potential energy ($U_\text{elas}$):
%The integration in Eq.~\ref{eq:spring-pot1} shows the same properties as in
%the work done by gravitational force that was previously shown in
%Section~\ref{section:gravitationalPE}:
\begin{definition}
  \begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
  \item When work done by the spring force is \emph{positive}, (i.e.\
    $W_\text{elas}>0$), there is a \emph{decrease} in spring potential energy
    (i.e.\ $U_\text{elas}<0$) by the same amount, while
  \item When work done by the spring force is \emph{negative}, (i.e.\
    $W_\text{elas}<0$), there is an \emph{increase} in spring potential energy
    (i.e.\ $U_\text{elas}>0$) by the same amount
  \item Positive work by the spring force transforms elastic potential energy
    into kinetic energy; negative work by the spring force transforms kinetic
    energy into elastic potential energy
  \item Work by the spring force is \emph{path independent}: $W_\text{elas}$
    depends on the end points $x_0$ and $x_1$, but not \emph{how} the object
    moves from $x_0$ to $x_1$
  \item Only work done by $\bm F_\text{elas}$ can affect $U_\text{elas}$
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}


\subsection{Electrostatic Force \& Electric Potential Energy}
Similar to the attractive force between masses, there is also a mutually
attractive or repulsive force between charged particles, given by
\textbf{Coulomb's law}:
\begin{equation}
  \bm F_\text{elec}=\frac{kq_1q_2}{r^2}\hat{\bm r}
\end{equation}
The integral is nearly identical to that for the gravitational force:
\begin{equation}
  W_\text{elec}=\int\bm F_\text{elec}\cdot\dl{\bm r}
  =kq_1q_2\int_{r_0}^{r_1}\frac{\dl r}{r^2}
    =-\frac{kq_1q_2} r\Big|^{r_2}_{r_1}=-\Delta U_\text{elec}
\end{equation}
where $U_\text{elec}$ is the \textbf{electric potential energy} that is stored
between the two point charges, defined as:
\begin{equation}
  U_\text{elec} = \frac{kq_1q_2}r
\end{equation}
Again, we see the same properties that we have observed for gravitational force
and spring force:
\begin{definition}
  \begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
  \item When work done by electrostatic force is \emph{positive}, (i.e.\
    $W_\text{elec}>0$), there is a \emph{decrease} in electric potential energy
    (i.e.\ $U_\text{elec}<0$) by the same amount;
  \item When work done by electrostatic force is \emph{negative}, (i.e.\
    $W_\text{elec}<0$), there is an \emph{increase} in electric potential energy
    (i.e.\ $U_\text{elec}>0$) by the same amount;
  \item Positive work by electrostatic force transforms electric potential
    energy into kinetic energy; negative work by electrostatic force transforms
    kinetic energy into electric potential energy;
  \item Work by electrostatic force is \emph{path independent}:
    $W_\text{elec}$ depends on the end points $r_0$ and $r_1$, but not
    \emph{how} the object moves from $r_0$ to $r_1$
  \item Only work done by electrostatic force $\bm F_\text{elec}$ can affect
    electric potential energy $U_\text{elec}$
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}



\section{Conservative Forces}

These forces are called \textbf{conservative forces}, which is a very short
list.
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
\item Gravitational force $\bm F_\text{grav}$
\item Spring force $\bm F_\text{elas}$
\item Electrostatic force $\bm F_\text{elec}$
\item Magnetic force $\bm F_\text{mag}$
\item Nuclear forces
\end{itemize}
Because they shared these common properties:
\begin{definition}
  \begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
  \item When work done by a conservative force is \emph{positive}:
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item There is a \emph{decrease} in the related potential energy by the
      same amount.
    \item That potential energy is transformed into the kinetic energy
    \end{itemize}
  \item When work done by a conservative force is \emph{negative}
    \begin{itemize}[nosep]
    \item There is an \emph{increase} in the related potential energy by the
      same amount
    \item The increase is because kinetic energy is transformed into potential
      energy by the work done
    \end{itemize}
    %\item Positive work by a conservative force transforms the related 
  %  potential energy into kinetic energy; negative work by a conservative force
  %  transforms kinetic energy into the related potential energy
  \item Work by the a conservative force is always path independent: it depends
    only on end points, but not \emph{how} it gets from one end point to the
    other
  \item Only work done by a conservative force can affect its related potential
    energy
  \end{itemize}
\end{definition}
If we integrate a conservative force ($\bm F_\text{con}$) to find the change in
the related potential energy $U$:
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta U=-\int_{\bm r_0}^{\bm r_1}\bm F_\text{con}\cdot\dl{\bm r}
\end{equation*}
Then by the fundamental theorem of calculus, any conservative forces
$\bm F_\text{con}$ must be the negative gradient of that related potential
energy:
\begin{important-equation}
  \bm F_\text{con}=-\nabla U=-\diffp Ux\iii-\diffp Uy\jjj-\diffp Uz\kkk
\end{important-equation}
In one-dimension, the gradient operator becomes just:
\begin{important-equation}
  F_\text{con}=-\diff Ux
\end{important-equation}
The direction of a conservative force \emph{always} decreases the potential
energy. (Pay attention to the negative sign. Students often forget it.)

\section{Non-Conservative Forces}
Conservative forces only account for a small list of known forces; the majority
of the common forces encountered in introductory physics courses are generally
\textbf{non-conservative}. They include, but not limited to:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item Applied force $\bm F_\text{app}$
\item Tension force $\bm F_\text{ten}$
\item Normal force $\bm F_\text{norm}$
\item Static and kinetic friction $\bm F_\text{fric}$
\item Aerodynamic lift $\bm F_\text{lift}$
\item Aerodynamic drag $\bm F_\text{drag}$
\end{itemize}
The work-energy principle (Eq~\ref{work-energy-theorem}) still applies for
non-conservative forces. However, the work done by non-conservative forces
differs from conservative forces in that:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item There is \textbf{no related potential energies}: the work done by a
  non-conservative force transform energy from one form of kinetic energy to
  another
\item The work is \textbf{path dependent}
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Work by Friction}
We can illustrate the work done by a non-conservative force in general, by
examining how work is done by static friction, using a standard multi-body
problem studied in dynamics. Two blocks with masses $m_1$ and $m_2$, stacked
vertically, move to the right without slipping by an external applied force
$\bm F_\text{app}$ applied to $m_2$, as shown in Figure~\ref{stacked1}.

The static friction at the interface between $m_1$ and $m_2$ is
$\bm F_\text{fric}$, which acts to the right on $m_1$ and to the left on $m_2$,
as required by the third law of motion. In the discussions on multi-body
dynamics in Chapter~\ref{chapter:dynamics}, we already know that mass $m_1$
accelerates to the right because of that static friction $\bm F_\text{fric}$.
Without that friction, $m_1$ would just slide off the back of $m_2$ when $\bm F$
is applied.

On $m_1$, the \emph{only} force doing work is $\bm F_\text{fric}$---both the
normal force $\bm F_{\text{norm},1}$ and gravitational force $\bm F_{g_1}$ are
perpendicular to motion. The work done by $\bm F_\text{fric}$ is
\emph{positive}. As $\bm F_\text{fric}$ accelerates $m_1$ to the right, it gains
kinetic energy, consistent with the work-energy principle
(Eq.~\ref{work-energy-theorem}). The change in kinetic energy in $m_1$ as it
moves from $x_0\to x_1$ is
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta K_1=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F_\text{fric}\dl x
\end{equation*}
%Note that since we are not assuming whether applied force $\bm F$ is
%constant, we cannot assume whether static friction is constant, and we cannot
On the bottom block $m_2$, as it accelerates to the right, applied force $\bm F$
does \emph{positive} work, while static friction $\bm F_\text{fric}$ does
\emph{negative} work.
%At a minimum, we conclude that energy is transferred
%from $m_1$ to $m_2$, but we can do a more thorough (although still very simple)
%analysis to find out how much.
Without static friction, the net force on $m_2$ would have just been $\bm F$,
and the change in kinetic energy moving from $x_0\to x_1$ to the right would
simply be:
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta K_2 = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} F_\text{app}\dl x
  %\quad\quad\text{(no friction)}
\end{equation*}
However, with static friction present, the gain in kinetic energy is reduced
due to the negative work that it does:
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta K_2 = \int_{x_0}^{x_1} F_\text{net}\dl x =
  \int_{x_0}^{x_1} (F_\text{app}-F_\text{fric})\dl x =
  \int_{x_0}^{x_1} F_\text{app}\dl x -
  \underbracket[1pt]{\int_{x_0}^{x_1} F_\text{fric}\dl x}_{\Delta K_1}
\end{equation*}
Work done by static friction reduced the kinetic energy of $m_2$ by the same
amount that is gained by $m_1$. Because the force does positive work on one
block and negative work on the other, We conclude that work done by static
friction transfers kinetic energy from one block to another.

Now, if the applied force $\bm F_\text{app}$ is large enough to overcome static
friction, then it will cause the $m_1$ to slide off $m_2$. We can use this
example to learn about the work done by kinetic friction. For this case, the
free-body diagrams in Figure~\ref{stacked-fbd} are still valid, but
$\bm F_\text{fric}$ now represents \emph{kinetic} friction instead of static
friction. As $\bm F_\text{fric}$ accelerates $m_1$ to the right, the gain in
kinetic energy as it moves from $x_0\to x_1$ is
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta K_1=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F_\text{fric}\dl x=\mu_km_1g(x_1-x_0)
\end{equation*}
There is no need to integrate because kinetic friction is constant regardless
of the applied force.

With kinetic friction, the bottom block $m_2$ will travel a longer distance,
from $x_0\to x_2$ where $x_2>x_1$, as it accelerates to the right.
Applied force $\bm F$ still does work, while kinetic friction
$\bm F_\text{fric}$ does negative work. The gain in kinetic energy is now
\begin{equation*}
  \Delta K_2 = \int_{x_0}^{x_2} F_\text{net}\dl x =
  \int_{x_0}^{x_2} (F_\text{app}-F_\text{fric})\dl x =
  \int_{x_0}^{x_2} F_\text{app}\dl x -
  \underbracket[1pt]{\mu_km_1g(x_2-x_0)}_{>\Delta K_1}
\end{equation*}
Negative work done by kinetic friction reduced the kinetic energy of $m_2$ by
the more than the kinetic energy gained by $m_1$. So were did the energy go?
A casual observation will show that as the two blocks slide against each other,
both of them are heated up, i.e.\ they increase in temperature. We therefore
conclude that work done by kinetic friction transfers kinetic energy from
one block to another, and also from kinetic energy into another form of
energy called thermal energy.

stored when the spring is stretched or compressed. But the random motion of
the air molecules in the container, the container itself, and the spring also
contribute to additional energy, called the object's \textbf{thermal energy}, or
\textbf{internal energy} $E_\text{thermal}$. The total thermal energy of a
system of molecules is the sum of all the kinetic and potential energies at the
molecular level:
\begin{equation}
  E_\text{thermal}=K_\text{mol.} + U_\text{mol.}
\end{equation}
As the name suggests, thermal energy has to do with how \emph{hot} an object
is, and so it is no surprise that $E_\text{thermal}$ is proportional to
molecules' \textbf{absolute temperature}, measured in \emph{kelvin}
(\si\kelvin). Discussions on thermal energy and the behaviour of gases and
solids are part of a large discipline within physics called
\textbf{thermodynamics}, but it is outside the scope of this book.
\begin{remark}
  For those who are keen to know, for a monatomic ideal gas like helium,
  thermal energy of the gas comes entirely from the kinetic energy from the
  translational motion of the molecules, given by:
  \begin{equation*}
    E_\text{thermal}=\dfrac32Nk_bT
  \end{equation*}
  where $N$ is the number of molecules, $k_b$ is the Boltzmann's constant,
  which is a universal constant governing thermodynamic behaviour. For a
  diatomic gas, thermal energy is given approximately by:
  \begin{equation*}
    E_\text{thermal}\approx\dfrac52Nk_bT
  \end{equation*}
  For solids with a perfect lattice-crystalline structure, like those seen in
  metals, %there are 3 degrees of translational freedom, and 3 degrees of
  %degrees of vibrational freedom, and therefore
  the thermal energy is:
  \begin{equation*}
    E_\text{thermal}\approx3Nk_bT
  \end{equation*}
  However, this approximation is only valid for very specific types of material
  structures. The key is that regardless of whether the material is solid,
  liquid or solid, thermal energy is \emph{always} a linear function of
  temperature $T$.
\end{remark}

\section{Law of Conservation of Energy}
We can now turn our attention to the single more recognizable and possibly the
most important concept in physics all together. The law of conservation of
energy is most often stated in a way that any lay person with no physics
background watching a movie may be familiar with:
\begin{definition}
  \textbf{Conservation of Energy (lay person's version):} In an isolated system,
  energy cannot be created or destroyed; it only changes in form.
\end{definition}
While this statement certainly is not incorrect---indeed, it is an insightful
statement that absolutely captures the \emph{essence} of how energy is
conserved---the actual law for the conservation of energy is more nuanced, and
a more tedious bookkeeping exercise:
\begin{definition}
  \textbf{Law of conservation of energy:} The change in the total energy of a
  system is equal to the net external work done to the system.
\end{definition}
We can express the law of conservation of energy with a simple equation:
\begin{important-equation}
  \Delta E_\text{sys}=W_\text{ext}
  \label{eq:energy-conservation-law}
\end{important-equation}
In order to understand how energy is conserved, we must understand the meaning
of a \emph{system}, what \emph{energies} are included, and how external work is
done from outside the system.



\subsection{System}

Before we can show how energy \emph{must} be conserved, we must first define
what a system is. A \emph{system} of objects is defined in the same way as in
solving dynamics problems, specifically, the multi-body problems studied in
Section~\ref{sec:multibody}: a predefined collection of objects that apply
forces on each other\footnote{This statement may be plainly obvious to some,
but not to many others: the third law of motion is at work here when objects
applies forces on each other.}. Because objects apply forces on each other,
they may also do work on each other. A system may be:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item\textbf{Isolated:} All the forces that act on the objects in the system
  act on each other (as required by the third law of motion) and are therefore
  \emph{internal}. There are either no external forces, or the external forces
  do not do any mechanical work.
\item\textbf{Open:} There are external forces, and they do mechanical work
  (``external work'') to the objects in the system. Depending on the sign of
  the external work, this mechanical work either adds energy to the system, or
  remove energy from the system.
\end{itemize}



\subsection{Energies of a System}

The system's energy depend on what objects are part of the system. They include
all the kinetic energies of objects $i$ that may be in motion, the potential
energies stored in the objects, as well as the internal/thermal energies of the
objects. Kinetic and potential energies at the macroscopic levels are 
collectively known as the \textbf{mechanical energy}, while kinetic and
potential energies in at the molecular/microscopic level is known as the
\textbf{thermal energy}.
\begin{important-equation}
  E_\text{sys}
  =\underbracket[1pt]{\sum K_i+\sum U_i}_\text{mechanical energy}+
  \sum E_\text{thermal}
  \label{eq:system-energy}
\end{important-equation}
When we solve problems with conservation of energy, we will have consider all
these energies. In this case, the \emph{change} in system energy must therefore
be the total change of all the energies in the systems:
\begin{important-equation}
  \Delta E_\text{sys}
  =\sum\Delta K_i+\sum\Delta U_i+\sum\Delta E_\text{thermal}
  \label{eq:system-energy-change}
\end{important-equation}
Substituting the expression from Eq.~\ref{eq:system-energy-change} into
Eq.~\ref{eq:energy-conservation-law}, we get the ``change'' in system energy:
\begin{equation}
  \sum\Delta K + \sum\Delta U + \sum\Delta E_\text{thermal}=W_\text{ext}
\end{equation}




\subsection{External Work}
\label{sec:ext-work}

The sign convention of the external work $W_\text{ext}$ is the same as how it
is defined in Section~\ref{sec:mechwork}. When external work is: 
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item\textbf{Positive}: work is done \text{to} the system by the objects in
  the surrounding; the system energy \emph{increases}
\item\textbf{Negative}: work is done \text{by} the system to its surrounding;
  the system energy \emph{decreases}
\end{itemize}
In an isolated system that does not interact with the outside (and therefore no
external work can be done to the system), the law of conservation of energy
further simplifies to
\begin{equation}
  \Delta E_\text{sys}=\sum\Delta K+\sum\Delta U+\sum\Delta E_\text{thermal}=0
\end{equation}
which perfectly reflects the ``layman's version'' of the law of conservation of
energy. If the total change is zero, no energy is created or destroyed.

\section{Setting Up Conservation of Energy Problems}
Before we are able to solve problems using conservation of energy, we first
need to set it up. There are a few steps to this:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=6pt]
\item\textbf{What objects form the system?} Ideally, we would like to include
  enough objects so that the system is isolated, but there are definitely
  situations where this is not possible, and we will have to calculate (or at
  least approximate) the amount of external work done.

\item\textbf{What forms are energy are associated with the objects in the
  system?} Every object that moves can have a kinetic energy. Every spring that
  can stretch or compress---or any object that can deform---can store potential
  energy, and every mass store gravitational potential energy with Earth.
  
\item\textbf{What forces act between the object (i.e.\ what are the internal
  forces)?} The internal forces, and the work that they do, will tell you how
  energy is transformed \emph{within} the system.
  
\item\textbf{What forces act from the outside (i.e.\ what are the external
  forces)? And are they doing any work to the system?} There are objects that
  are not inside the system. When they apply forces that to objects that
  \emph{are} in the system, they can also do work on objects, that work is the
  external work. We follow the discussion in Section~\ref{sec:ext-work}.
\end{enumerate}



\section{Gravitational Systems}

The first system that we study is the gravitational system.

\subsection{Free Fall}

Assuming that there are no friction, drag (see Section~\ref{sec:drag}) or other
damping forces, a free-falling object of mass $m$, shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:earth}, forms an isolated system with Earth.
\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.5]
    \draw[mass] (7.75,0) arc (75:105:30);
    \draw[mass] (0,7) circle (.2) node[right=3]{$m$};
    \draw[vector,red] (0,7)--+(0,-2) node[below=-2]{$\bm F_\text{grav}$};
    \draw[vector,red] (0,.5)--+(0,2) node[above=-2]{$\bm F_\text{grav}$};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{Isolated system with a mass and Earth}
  \label{fig:earth}
\end{figure}
This system consists of only Earth and the object, and therefore the energy of
the system is the kinetic energy of the mass ($K$) and the gravitational
potential energy ($U_\text{grav}$) stored between the mass and Earth.
\begin{remark}
  $U_\text{grav}$ is often called ``the gravitational potential energy of the
  object''. Strictly speaking, this is incorrect; this is a topic that will be
  studied in more detail in Chapter~\ref{chapter:gravity}. However, this will
  not affect how the calculations are done, and in the opinion of the author,
  poses no major conceptual issue. You are, of course, welcome to disagree.
\end{remark}
Since this is a free-fall, by definition, the only force that acts in the
system is the gravitational force $\bm F_\text{grav}$, which---by the third law
of motion---acts on \emph{both} the mass and Earth, and is therefore
\emph{internal} to the system. There are no other forces. Consequently, there
are no external forces, the system is isolated.

If the object falls \emph{towards} Earth, the gravitational force does positive
work on both the mass as well as Earth. Due to the mass of Earth, its
displacement due to this gravitational force is laughably insignificant. We
will therefore only focus on the motion of the mass. Since gravity is
conservative, the positive work done transforms gravitational potential energy
into kinetic energy of the mass by the same amount, and since
$\bm F_\text{grav}$ is an internal force, the change in the system's energy is
zero:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta K + \Delta U_\text{grav}=0
\end{equation}
Likewise, if the object is moving \emph{away} from Earth, then gravitational
force does negative work on both the mass as well as Earth. The negative work
transforms kinetic energy into gravitational potential energy by the same
amount. In either case, the above equation still holds.

In practice, we can use the following equation for problem solving:
\begin{equation}
  \underbracket[1pt]{K+U_\text{grav}}_\text{initial state}
  =\underbracket[1pt]{K'+U_\text{grav}'}_\text{final state}
\end{equation}
where the ``initial state'' and ``final state'' represent two points in time
during the free fall.

\begin{example}[Energy conservation in 1D free-fall]
  \label{eg:1d-freefall}
  A ball is thrown direction \emph{upwards} with an initial speed $v$ from the
  balcony of a tall building, at a height $h$ above the ground. If friction and
  drag can be ignored, with what speed $v'$ would it hit the ground?
  
  \textbf{Solution:} This is a simple problem usually solved using kinematics,
  but we will use conservation of energy instead. Since there is no friction
  and drag, total energy is conserved until the moment when the ball hits the
  ground. Setting $h=0$ at ground level:
  \begin{displaymath}
    \underbracket[1pt]{K + U_\text{grav}}_\text{thrown} =
    \underbracket[1pt]{K'+\cancel{U_\text{grav}'}}_\text{hitting the ground}
  \end{displaymath}
  Substituting the expressions for $K$ and $U_\text{grav}$, we have:
  %into the above equation,
  \begin{displaymath}
    \frac12 mv^2 + mgh = \frac12 mv'^2
  \end{displaymath}
  Cancelling mass terms, then solving for $v'$, we have the final expression:
  \begin{displaymath}
    v'= \sqrt{v^2+2gh}
  \end{displaymath}
  Our answer is---as one would expect---identical to using one-dimensional
  kinematic equations, but with some subtle differences:
  \begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
  \item This is a scalar problem, so we didn't have to define a positive
    direction, as we would have done in 1D vector problems. We actually
    don't know explicitly the direction of the ball's motion when it hits the
    ground, although it is plainly obvious that it falls downwards.
  \item We cannot find out how long it took to hit the ground.
  \item All we know is the speeds at the end points; we do not know how velocity
    changes during its motion.
  \end{itemize}
  We would have arrived at the same answer had the ball been thrown downwards
  instead.
\end{example}
Let's try something \emph{slightly} more difficult for a bit more insight. The
advantages of using the conservation of energy becomes more apparent for more
complex kinematic problems.
\begin{example}[Energy conservation in projectile motion]
  \label{eg:projectile-energy-conservation}
  A projectile is launched with an upward angle of $\theta$ and an initial
  speed of $v$ from a height $h$ above the ground. If friction and drag is
  negligible, with what speed would it hit the ground?
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.7]
      \draw[thick] (-1,0)--(0,0)--(0,-2)--(8,-2);
      \draw[dashed,thick,domain=0:6.8,samples=20] plot(\x,{-(.5*\x-1.42)^2+2});
      \draw[vector,red,rotate=52] (0,0)--+(1.5,0) node[pos=1.1]{$\bm v$};
      \draw (0,0)--(1.5,0);
      \draw[axes] (1,0) arc (0:52:1) node[midway,right]{$\theta$};
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{4pt}\textbf{Solution:} Again, since there is no friction and drag,
  the total energy is conserved between when the ball is thrown, to the moment
  just before it hits the ground:
  \begin{displaymath}
    \underbracket[1pt]{K + U_\text{grav}}_\text{thrown} =
    \underbracket[1pt]{K'+\cancel{U_\text{grav}'}}_\text{hitting the ground}
  \end{displaymath}
  Setting $h=0$ at ground level and substituting the expressions for $K$ and
  $U_\text{grav}$ into the above equation, as we did in the previous example, we
  can cancel the mass terms, and solve for $v'$:
  \begin{displaymath}
    \frac12 mv^2 + mgh = \frac12 mv'^2\quad\longrightarrow\quad
    \boxed{v'= \sqrt{v^2+2gh}}
  \end{displaymath}
  This is, of course, the exact same answer as the previous example. Solving
  this problem using 2D kinematics require us to split the problem into
  horizontal ($x$) and vertical ($y$) directions. We would have to find the
  vertical velocity when the ball hits the ground, then combine with the
  horizontal velocity to find the \emph{speed}. Using conservation of energy
  allows us to solve a scalar problem instead of a vector problem allowed us to
  solve this problem is a fraction of the time, and without worrying about
  whether we have used the correct vector directions.
\end{example}
In the next section, we will see that there are still examples for when the
only way to solve a problem is using conservation of energy.



\subsection{Arbitrary Ramp}

Assuming that there is no friction, drag or other damping forces present,
energy is also conserved for an object sliding down an arbitrarily-shaped ramp.
\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.9]
    \draw[thick] (0,4) to[out=-30,in=180] (3,1) to[out=0,in=180] (5,3)
    to[out=0,in=170] (8,0) to[out=-10,in=180] (10,0);
    \draw[mass,rotate around={-60.5:(1,2.93)}] (1,2.93) rectangle +(.6,.6);
    \fill[red] (1.4,2.8) circle (.08);
    \draw[vector,red] (1.4,2.8)--+(0,-1.5) node[left]{$\bm F_\text{grav}$};
    \draw[vector,red,rotate around={30:(1.4,2.8)}] (1.4,2.8)--+(1.4,0)
    node[right]{$\bm F_\text{norm}$};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{A mass sliding along an arbitrary ramp is an isolated system.}
  \label{fig:ramp-system}
\end{figure}
Like the free-fall problems, this system also consists of only Earth and the
object. Therefore the system energies are the kinetic energy of the mass ($K$)
and the gravitational potential energy ($U_\text{grav}$) stored between the mass
and Earth. In this case, there are two \emph{internal} forces acting between
the mass and Earth:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item Normal force $\bm F_\text{norm}$ at the interface due to the contact
  between both objects are, and 
\item Gravitational force $\bm F_\text{grav}$ between the centres of mass of the
  two objects
\end{itemize}
There are no external forces, therefore no external work. Like a free-fall,
this is also an isolated system. The sum of the kinetic energy
and gravitational potential energy ($U_\text{grav}$) is constant, i.e.:
\begin{equation}
  \Delta K+\Delta U_\text{grav}=0
\end{equation}
As the mass slides, only gravitational force is doing work, transforming energy
between gravitational potential energy and kinetic energy. ($\bm F_\text{norm}$
is always perpendicular to motion, therefore it does not do work.) The shape of
the ramp does not matter, only the initial and final height relative to the
reference.


\begin{example}[Energy conservation along an arbitrary ramp]
  A block slides down a arbitrarily shaped ramp with an initial speed $v$ from
  a height $h$ above the ground, already shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ramp-system}.
  If friction and drag can be ignored, with what speed $v'$ would it be when the
  block reaches the ground?
  %\begin{center}
  %  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.7]
  %    \draw[thick] (0,4) to[out=-30,in=180] (3,1) to[out=0,in=180] (5,3)
  %    to[out=0,in=170] (8,0) to[out=-10,in=180] (10,0);
  %    \draw[mass,rotate around={-60.5:(1,2.93)}] (1,2.93) rectangle +(.6,.6);
  %    \draw[vector,rotate around={-60:(1.4,2.8)}] (1.4,2.8)--+(1.4,0)
  %    node[right]{$\bm v$};
  %  \end{tikzpicture}
  %\end{center}
  %\vspace{-.1in}
  
  \textbf{Solution:} The set up for this problem is exactly the same as for
  the free-fall examples. Since there is no friction and drag, system energy is
  conserved throughout the entire motion of the block. Setting $h=0$ at ground
  level, conservation of energy equation is:
  \begin{displaymath}
    \underbracket[1pt]{K + U_\text{grav}}_\text{start} =
    \underbracket[1pt]{K' + \cancel{U_\text{grav}'}}_\text{end}
  \end{displaymath}
  Once again, we substitute the expressions for $K$ and $U_\text{grav}$ into
  the equation. We can then cancel the mass terms, and solve for $v'$:
  \begin{displaymath}
    \frac12 mv^2 + mgh = \frac12 mv'^2
    \quad\longrightarrow\quad
    v'= \sqrt{v^2+2gh}
  \end{displaymath}
  Our answer is, of course, identical to the free-fall problems in Examples
  \ref{eg:1d-freefall} and \ref{eg:projectile-energy-conservation}. Using
  conservation of energy, this problem is no more difficult than the simplest
  1D kinematic equations. But the most important observation is that this
  problem is \textbf{impossible to solve using kinematics and dynamics, because
    we were never told what the shape of the ramp is}! Using conservation of
  energy is really the only viable method to solving this problem.
\end{example}

\subsection{Horizontal Spring-Mass System}
In a horizontal spring-mass system, a mass $m$ sits on a level horizontal
surface. One end of a spring is attached to the mass, and the other to a fixed
point, thus allowing it to vibrate in the horizontal direction, as shown in
Figure~\ref{fig:hspring-mass}.
\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.2]
    \draw[mass] (5,.5) rectangle (6,1.5);
    \draw[thick,decorate,
      decoration={coil,amplitude=6,aspect=.5,segment length=6}] (0,1)--(5,1);
    \fill[pattern=north east lines] (6.5,.5)--(6.5,.3)--(-.2,.3)
    --(-.2,2)--(0,2)--(0,.5)--cycle;
    \draw[very thick] (0,2)--(0,.5)--(6.5,.5);
    \begin{scope}[vector,red]
      \draw (5.5,1)--+(0,-1) node[below]{$\bm F_\text{grav}$};
      \draw (5.5,1)--+(0,1) node[above]{$\bm F_\text{norm}$};
      \draw (5.5,1)--+(-1,0) node[above]{$\bm F_\text{elas}$};
    \end{scope}
    \fill[red] (5.5,1) circle (.06);
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{A horizontal spring-mass system without friction or drag is a
  closed system}
  \label{fig:hspring-mass}
\end{figure}
Assuming that there are no friction, drag or other damping forces present, this
horizontal spring-mass system is an isolated system: it consists of the mass and
the spring. (We can include Earth as part of the system, but it is not
necessary.) Therefore the system's total energy consists of the kinetic energy
of the mass ($K$) and the elastic potential energy stored in the spring
($U_\text{elas}$). Several \emph{external} forces do, in fact, act on the
system: the gravitational force ($\bm F_\text{grav}$) and normal force
($\bm F_\text{norm}$) both act on the mass. However, these forces do not do any
work because they are perpendicular to the motion of the mass. Therefore there
is no external work, and the total energy of the system is constant, i.e.:
\begin{important-equation}
  \Delta K + \Delta U_\text{elas}=0
  \quad\text{(frictionless horizontal spring-mass)}
\end{important-equation}
In practice, we would solve the following problem:
\begin{align}
  \nonumber
  \underbracket[1pt]{K+U_\text{elas}}_\text{initial state} &=
  \underbracket[1pt]{K'+U_\text{elas}'}_\text{final state}\\
  \frac12mv^2+\frac12kx^2 &= \frac12m(v')^2+\frac12k(x')^2
\end{align}
where, like the gravitational problems, the ``initial state'' and ``final
state'' represent to points in time during the motion of the spring-mass system.

\begin{example}[Toy Cart]
  A toy cart with a mass $m$
  %$\SI{.25}{\kilo\gram}$
  travels along a frictionless horizontal track and collides head on with a
  spring with a spring constant $k$.
  %of \SI{155}{\newton\per\metre}.
  If the spring is compressed by a distance $x$,
  %\SI{6.0}{\centi\metre},
  how fast is the cart initially travelling?
  \begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}
      \fill[blue!30] rectangle (10,-.3);
      \draw (0,0)--(10,0);
      \draw[fill=brown] (.5,.2) rectangle (3,.5) node[midway,above=3]{$m$};
      \draw[fill=gray] (1,.2) circle (.2);
      \draw[fill=gray] (2.5,.2) circle (.2);
      \fill (1,.2) circle (.06);
      \fill (2.5,.2) circle (.06);
      \draw[vectors] (3,.35)--(4.5,.35) node[above]{$v$};
      \draw[pattern=north east lines] (9.3,0) rectangle (10,1.7);
      \draw[ultra thick,decorate,
        decoration={aspect=.4,segment length=1.5mm, amplitude=2mm, coil}]
      (6.3,.4)--(9.3,.4) node[midway,above=3]{spring};
    \end{tikzpicture}
  \end{center}
  \vspace{.2in}\textbf{Solution:} Even when the mass isn't always attached to
  the spring, energy can still be conserved. In this very simple case, we
  start with the conservation of energy equation, noting that $U_\text{elas}$
  (initial elastic potential energy) and $K'$ (final kinetic energy) are both
  zero:
  \begin{equation*}
    K+\cancel{U_\text{elas}}= \cancel{K'}+U_\text{elas}'
    %\frac12mv^2+\frac12kx^2 &= \frac12m(v')^2+\frac12k(x')^2
  \end{equation*}
  We can substitute expressions for $K$ and $U_\text{elas}$, then cancel the
  $\frac12$ terms, and solve for initial speed $v$:
  \begin{equation*}
    \frac12mv^2 = \frac12k(x')^2
    \quad\longrightarrow\quad
    v=x'\sqrt{\frac km}
  \end{equation*}
\end{example}




\subsection{Vertical Spring-Mass System}
In an ideal vertical spring-mass system, a mass is suspended vertically by a
massless spring, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:vspring-mass}. The mass is allowed
to oscillate vertically. % with no friction, drag or other damping forces.
\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}
    \draw[mass] (.5,1.5) rectangle (1.5,2.5);% node[midway]{$m$};
    \draw[thick,decorate,decoration={
        aspect=.5,segment length=7, amplitude=5,coil}] (1,5)--(1,2.5);

\end{align}

\end{align}

\section{Accounting for Changing Thermal Energy}
Energy is \emph{always} conserved as long as the system is defined properly.
But doing that is not always simple, or even possible. Consider a system that
consists of a mass, a spring, Earth and all the air molecules inside a
perfectly insulated box, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:isolated-with-thermal}.
\begin{figure}[ht]
  \centering
  \begin{tikzpicture}[scale=.8,thick]
    \fill[pattern=north east lines] rectangle (5,4);
    \draw rectangle (5,4);
    \draw[fill=blue!5] (.2,.2) rectangle (4.8,3.8);
    \draw[thick,decorate,
      decoration={aspect=.3,segment length=2mm, amplitude=2.5mm, coil}]
    (2.5,3.8)--(2.5,2.25) node[midway,right=6]{$k$};
    \draw[mass] (2,2.25) rectangle (3,1.25) node[midway]{$m$};
  \end{tikzpicture}
  \caption{In this isolated system, mechanical energy decreases in time while
    internal/thermal energy increases.}
  \label{fig:isolated-with-thermal}
\end{figure}
The energies of this system include the kinetic energy of the mass ($K$), the
gravitational potential energy ($U_\text{grav}$) between the mass and Earth, the
elastic potential energy ($U_\text{elas}$) stored in the spring, as well as the
thermal energy ($E_\text{thermal}$) of the air molecules, the mass, the
insulated box, and the spring. This is an isolated system with no interaction
with its surrounding: Gravitational force, spring force, drag and and friction
that is present are all \emph{internal} forces. Therefore the total energy of
the system is conserved:% even as the mass stops moving:
\begin{equation*}
  K + E_\text{thermal}+U_\text{grav}+U_\text{elas}=\text{constant}
\end{equation*}
As the mass vibrates up and down:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=6pt]
\item Work done by gravitational force converts between gravitational potential
  energy and kinetic energy
\item Work done by the spring force converts between elastic potential energy
  and kinetic energy
\item Work done by friction and drag is always negative on the mass, therefore
  the mass eventually slows down, and its kinetic energy is transformed into
  the thermal energy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Advantages and Disadvantages of Conservation of Energy}
If solving motion problems using the law of energy conservation is so much
easier, why don't we use it all the time?

Advantages:
\begin{itemize}
\item Scalar: there are no vectors and no need to define directions
\item Algebra: calculus can be avoided in most cases
\item Fast!
\end{itemize}

Disadvantages:
\begin{itemize}
\item Cannot tell you direction of motion
\item Cannot tell you the total time
\item Cannot tell you the acceleration of objects in many cases
\item For spring-mass and pendulum systems: cannot tell you why
  object oscillates
\end{itemize}



\section{Power}

\textbf{ANOTHER SECTION THAT HAS VERY LITTLE INFORMATION, BECAUSE I DON'T SPEND
  THAT MUCH TIME DISCUSSING THIS IN CLASS. THE BOOK, HOWEVER, SHOULD HAVE MORE
  INFORMATION.}

\textbf{Power} is the \emph{rate} at which work is done, i.e.\ the rate at
which energy is being transformed. In engineering, power is often more critical
than the actual amount of work done.

\textbf{Instantaneous power} $P(t)$ generated by a force is defined as the time
derivative of work done, or the derivative of the change in the amount of a
specific form of energy:
\begin{important-equation}
  P(t) = \diff Wt = \diff Et
\end{important-equation}
Work can be either the work done by a specific force, or the net work done on
an object, depending on the situation.

The \textbf{average power} $\overline P$ is defined as the total work over a
\emph{finite} time interval:
\begin{important-equation}
  \overline P(t) = \frac W{\Delta t}=
  \frac{\int_{t_0}^t P(t)\dl t}{\Delta t}
\end{important-equation}
If a force is used to push an object at a constant velocity, the instantaneous
power produced by the force is:
\begin{equation}
  P=\diff Wt=\frac{\bm F\cdot\dl{\bm x}}{\dl t}
  =\bm F\cdot\diff{\bm x}t
\end{equation}
\begin{important-equation}
  P(t)=\bm F(t)\cdot\bm v(t)
\end{important-equation}
One important application of the equation is in aerodynamics. When an object
moves through air, the applied force must overcome air resistance (drag force),
which is proportional with $v^2$ Therefore ``aerodynamic power'' must scale
with $v^3$ (i.e.\ doubling your speed requires $2^3=8$ times more power). This
is most important when aerodynamic forces dominate.
